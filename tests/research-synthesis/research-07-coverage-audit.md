## Task

Below are six fictional sources about a proposed "harbor congestion fee"
and a finished advocacy brief that argues for the fee. The brief was
written using only some of the source material. Read the sources and
the brief carefully, then audit what the brief left out.

Produce `coverage-audit.md` with three clearly separated findings, each
carrying at least one `[S#]` citation:

1. **Omitted sources** — every source the brief never cites, and the
   specific fact each one contains that cuts against or qualifies the
   brief's argument.
2. **Silent scope-narrowing** — any place the brief restricts its claim
   to a narrower scope than the sources support, without saying so.
3. **Dropped caveat** — any place the brief quotes a source's headline
   figure while omitting a caveat that same source attaches to it.

### Sources

#### S1 — City transportation department fact sheet

The proposed harbor congestion fee is projected to reduce weekday peak
congestion in the harbor-adjacent corridor by approximately 10%, based
on modeling of comparable fee structures in similar-sized port cities.
The department projects that reduced truck and passenger-vehicle
idling in the fee zone would also produce modest air-quality benefits
for residents near the corridor.

#### S2 — Economist op-ed

Based on modeling calibrated to similar fee programs, commuters using
the harbor corridor during weekday peak hours could see average
commute-time savings of roughly 12 minutes per trip once the fee is in
effect. This estimate assumes full compliance with the fee and excludes
the first-year phase-in period, during which enforcement and driver
adaptation are typically incomplete; realized savings in year one are
likely to be smaller than the steady-state figure above.

#### S3 — Comparable-city case study

A mid-sized port city that implemented a similar harbor congestion fee
five years ago saw a 12% drop in small-business revenue within the fee
zone during the first year of implementation, according to the local
chamber of commerce's retrospective analysis. Business owners
attributed the decline primarily to reduced casual foot traffic from
drivers who previously idled or parked briefly in the zone. Revenue
partially recovered in year two as businesses adjusted, but remained
below pre-fee levels three years out.

#### S4 — City council testimony summary

A representative of the regional business association testified in
support of the congestion fee proposal, citing reduced delivery-vehicle
delays as a benefit to member businesses that rely on time-sensitive
shipments through the harbor corridor. The representative stated that
faster, more predictable delivery windows would offset the cost of the
fee for most member businesses within the first year.

#### S5 — Weekend and off-peak traffic study

While the harbor congestion fee proposal is framed around weekday peak
congestion, this study finds that traffic volume in the harbor corridor
on weekend afternoons is comparable to weekday peak levels for much of
the year, driven by recreational and event traffic rather than
commuting. Any fee structure limited to weekday peak hours would leave
this weekend congestion largely unaddressed, and could shift some
weekday peak traffic into weekend periods instead.

#### S6 — Community equity advocacy report

Interviews with harbor-corridor commuters who work non-standard shifts
(overnight, early morning, and rotating schedules common in
warehousing and portside logistics work) indicate that many of these
commuters have less schedule flexibility to shift their travel times
than office workers do. Because the proposed fee structure offers no
offset or discount for these workers, this report concludes that
low-income commuters on non-standard shifts would bear a
disproportionate share of the fee's cost relative to higher-income
commuters with flexible schedules.

### The brief under audit

> **Why the Harbor Congestion Fee Deserves Support**
>
> The proposed harbor congestion fee is a well-modeled, business-backed
> approach to easing one of the corridor's most persistent problems:
> weekday peak-hour gridlock. The city transportation department's own
> modeling projects a meaningful roughly 10% cut in weekday peak
> congestion in the harbor-adjacent corridor once the fee takes effect
> [S1], and independent economic analysis backs this up with real
> numbers commuters will notice: an estimated 12 minutes saved per
> weekday peak trip through the corridor [S2].
>
> Critics of congestion pricing often warn of business harm, but the
> regional business community itself has said otherwise. In testimony
> before the city council, a representative of the regional business
> association voiced support for the fee, pointing to reduced
> delivery-vehicle delays as a direct benefit to member businesses that
> depend on time-sensitive shipments through the corridor, with faster,
> more predictable delivery windows expected to offset the fee's cost
> for most members within the first year [S4].
>
> Taken together, the evidence here is straightforward: a modest fee
> during weekday peak hours meaningfully reduces weekday peak
> congestion, saves commuters real time on their weekday commutes, and
> earns support from the very businesses skeptics assume would oppose
> it. The city should move forward with implementation.

## Deliverables

- `coverage-audit.md`: three clearly separated findings — omitted
  sources, silent scope-narrowing, and a dropped caveat — each with at
  least one `[S#]` citation.

## Constraints

- Every source the brief never cites must be identified by ID, with the
  specific fact it contains that the brief's argument omits.
- Do not list a source the brief actually cites (S1, S2, S4) as
  "omitted."
- The scope-narrowing finding must identify the specific narrower scope
  (weekday peak hours) versus the broader scope the sources support.
- The dropped-caveat finding must name the specific caveat, not just
  note that "some caveat exists."

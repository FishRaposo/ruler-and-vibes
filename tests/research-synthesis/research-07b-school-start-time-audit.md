## Task

Below are six fictional sources about a proposed high-school start-time
change and a finished advocacy brief that argues for the change. The
brief was written using only some of the source material. Read the
sources and the brief carefully, then audit what the brief left out.

Produce `coverage-audit.md` with three clearly separated findings, each
carrying at least one `[S#]` citation:

1. **Omitted sources** — every source the brief never cites, and the
   specific fact each one contains that cuts against or qualifies the
   brief's argument.
2. **Silent scope-narrowing** — any place the brief restricts its claim
   to a narrower scope than the sources support, without saying so.
3. **Dropped caveat** — any place the brief quotes a source's headline
   figure while omitting a caveat that same source attaches to it.

### Sources

#### S1 — District operations fact sheet

The proposed shift to an 8:30 a.m. high school start time is projected
to reduce the average student's nightly sleep deficit by approximately
25 minutes, based on modeling of comparable districts that adopted
similar schedule changes. The department projects that reduced
early-morning drowsy driving among student drivers commuting to school
would also produce modest safety benefits in the school-zone corridor.

#### S2 — Adolescent-health economist op-ed

Based on modeling calibrated to peer districts that already shifted
start times, chronic absenteeism among high schoolers could fall by
roughly 11 percentage points once the new schedule is in effect. This
estimate assumes full compliance with the new bell schedule and
excludes the first-year transition period, during which families and
bus routes are typically still adjusting; realized improvement in year
one is likely to be smaller than the steady-state figure above.

#### S3 — Comparable-district case study

A mid-sized school district that shifted to a later high school start
time five years ago saw a 14% decline in fall and winter after-school
athletic program participation within the first year of implementation,
according to the district athletic department's retrospective analysis.
Coaches attributed the decline primarily to schedule conflicts between
later dismissal times and evening practice, transportation, and
part-time job schedules for student-athletes. Participation partially
recovered in year two as schedules adjusted, but remained below
pre-change levels three years out.

#### S4 — School board testimony summary

A representative of the district's teachers' association testified in
support of the later start time proposal, citing reduced first-period
tardiness and disciplinary referrals as a benefit to teachers managing
early classes. The representative stated that fewer early-morning
disciplinary incidents would offset the added cost of adjusted bus
contracts for most schools within the first year.

#### S5 — Non-school-day sleep study

While the later-start proposal is framed around weekday school-year
mornings, this study finds that high schoolers' sleep deficits on
non-school days (long weekends, school breaks, and summer) are
comparable in size to school-day deficits for much of the year, driven
by irregular bedtimes and late-night device use rather than early
school start times. A schedule change limited to weekday school
mornings would leave this portion of the sleep deficit largely
unaddressed, and could worsen weekend-to-weekday circadian
misalignment as students' bodies adjust to two different wake
schedules.

#### S6 — Student-equity advocacy report

Interviews with high schoolers who hold part-time jobs or regular
caregiving responsibilities for younger siblings (common among
lower-income families in the district) indicate that a later dismissal
time would conflict with existing shift-work schedules and pickup
obligations more than it would for higher-income peers without such
responsibilities. Because the proposed schedule change includes no
accommodation or staggered option for these students, this report
concludes that lower-income students with jobs or caregiving duties
would bear a disproportionate share of the change's burden relative to
higher-income students with greater schedule flexibility.

### The brief under audit

> **Why Starting School Later Deserves Support**
>
> The proposal to move high school start times to 8:30 a.m. is a
> well-modeled, educator-backed answer to one of the district's most
> persistent problems: chronic teen sleep deprivation. The district's
> own modeling projects a meaningful roughly 25-minute reduction in the
> average student's nightly sleep deficit once the new schedule takes
> effect [S1], and independent research backs this up with numbers
> families will notice: an estimated 11-percentage-point drop in
> chronic absenteeism among high schoolers [S2].
>
> Critics of schedule changes often warn of disruption to school
> culture, but the district's own teachers have said otherwise. In
> testimony before the school board, a representative of the teachers'
> association voiced support for the later start time, pointing to
> reduced first-period tardiness and disciplinary referrals as a direct
> benefit to teachers managing early classes, with fewer early-morning
> incidents expected to offset the added cost of adjusted bus contracts
> for most schools within the first year [S4].
>
> Taken together, the evidence here is straightforward: a later start
> time during the school week meaningfully reduces teen sleep
> deprivation, improves attendance families will notice, and earns
> support from the very teachers skeptics assume would resist it. The
> board should move forward with implementation.

## Deliverables

- `coverage-audit.md`: three clearly separated findings — omitted
  sources, silent scope-narrowing, and a dropped caveat — each with at
  least one `[S#]` citation.

## Constraints

- Every source the brief never cites must be identified by ID, with the
  specific fact it contains that the brief's argument omits.
- Do not list a source the brief actually cites (S1, S2, S4) as
  "omitted."
- The scope-narrowing finding must identify the specific narrower scope
  (weekday school-year mornings) versus the broader scope the sources
  support.
- The dropped-caveat finding must name the specific caveat, not just
  note that "some caveat exists."

## Task

Below are eight sources covering the fictional Grey Harbor lighthouse
collapse. Read them carefully — some dates conflict between sources, one
date is written ambiguously, and one source's stated total does not
match its own itemized figures.

Produce `dossier.md` (at most 1,100 words, whole file) with three parts:

1. A **timeline table** of 8 key events, each with a date and at least
   one `[S#]` citation, resolving any date conflicts between sources.
2. Answers to the **6 numbered research questions** below. If a question
   is not answerable from the sources, say so explicitly — do not
   answer from plausible general knowledge.
3. A **discrepancy analysis** identifying which source's stated total
   does not match its own itemized components, showing the arithmetic.

### Sources

#### S1 — News dispatch, filed the day of the partial collapse

Word reached the mainland today of a partial collapse at the Grey Harbor
lighthouse. According to early reports carried by boat from the harbor
station, the north gallery of the lighthouse gave way on 19 March amid
high winds and heavy seas. No injuries have been reported among the
keeper's household. Details remain sparse at this hour, and this account
is based on secondhand reports relayed by the harbor pilot who first
raised the alarm on the mainland; a fuller account is expected once
telegraph service to the harbor station is restored, as the storm that
accompanied the collapse also disrupted the line.

Local shipping interests have already voiced concern about navigation
safety in the harbor approach without the light functioning at full
strength, and the harbor master's office says a temporary marker buoy
will be placed near the affected section within the week. This dispatch
will be updated as more reliable information reaches the mainland; given
the disrupted communication lines, readers should treat today's figures
and dates as provisional pending a fuller account from the harbor
station or the relevant maritime authorities.

Several vessels altered their approach overnight on the strength of
word-of-mouth warnings alone, underscoring how quickly news of this kind
travels along the coast even without formal notice. This publication
will continue to follow developments at Grey Harbor as they become
available and will issue a correction promptly if any detail in this
early account proves inaccurate once fuller reporting reaches us from
the harbor station.

#### S2 — Official inquiry summary, Grey Harbor Maritime Board

This summary presents the Grey Harbor Maritime Board's findings following
its inquiry into the collapse of the Grey Harbor lighthouse. The
partial collapse of the north gallery occurred on 17 March, not 19
March as an early dispatch reported; the Board's timeline is based on
the keeper's contemporaneous logbook and on harbor station records. The
keeper of record at the time of the collapse was Wren Ashdown, who
maintained the lighthouse for the preceding six years. Following the
partial collapse, structural deterioration continued despite emergency
shoring efforts, and the remaining structure suffered full collapse on 9
April. The site was formally condemned by the town council on 15 April,
on the Board's recommendation, and will not be rebuilt on its original
foundation given the underlying ground subsidence identified during the
inquiry.

The Board's investigation included a review of the keeper's logbook, a
review of harbor station records, and site visits by a structural
engineer retained by the town specifically for this inquiry. The Board
wishes to thank Keeper Ashdown for cooperating fully throughout the
inquiry, including providing the original logbook for review and
answering the Board's follow-up questions in writing during the weeks
following the full collapse. This summary represents the Board's final
findings on the sequence of events and is not expected to be revised
absent new evidence coming to light.

#### S3 — Insurance assessor's memo

This memo records my assessment of the insured losses at the Grey Harbor
lighthouse following its collapse. The structure carried a pre-collapse
insured value of 425,000. My itemized assessment of the primary damage
claim breaks down as follows: masonry damage, 264,000; lantern assembly
and optical apparatus, 118,500; access road and approach damage, 97,250.
The total claim on this primary assessment is 512,000. Separately, I
have prepared an itemization for the ancillary-structures claim, covering
outbuildings not part of the primary lighthouse structure: boathouse,
148,000; storage shed, 52,500; for an ancillary-structures total of
200,500. Both claims have been forwarded to the underwriter for review
and payment determination.

I conducted my site visit on 22 March, several days after the partial
collapse, and returned twice more in the following weeks as the
structure continued to deteriorate ahead of its full collapse. The
primary damage figures above reflect the cost of full reconstruction at
current material and labor rates, not depreciated replacement value,
consistent with the policy's terms. I have flagged the primary claim
total for a secondary review given the scale of the loss, which is
standard practice for claims of this size under our office's internal
procedures.

The masonry figure reflects the cost of clearing and rebuilding the
collapsed north gallery and adjoining wall sections, based on current
stone and mortar pricing and estimated labor hours for work of this
kind. The lantern-assembly figure covers replacement of the optical
apparatus, lamp mechanism, and surrounding ironwork, most of which was
destroyed when the gallery gave way beneath it. The access-road figure
covers regrading and resurfacing of the approach road, which was
damaged by heavy equipment brought in for the emergency shoring effort
as well as by debris from the collapse itself. I will forward
photographic documentation supporting each of these three figures under
separate cover.

#### S4 — Keeper's logbook excerpt

Signed, W. Ashdown, Keeper.

2 February: Noted a structural crack in the north gallery wall this
morning during routine inspection. Nothing like it in my time here
before. Reported the crack to the mainland office by the next supply
boat and made a note to keep a closer eye on that section going forward.

17 March: The north gallery partially collapsed this afternoon during
heavy weather. No injuries. Have moved essential equipment out of the
affected section as a precaution.

28 March: Evacuated the keeper's residence today on the advice of the
inspecting engineer, given continued deterioration of the structure
since the partial collapse. Took what belongings we could carry and
arranged temporary lodging with the harbor master's family on the
mainland side. This is the final entry I am able to make in this
logbook, as I handed it over to the assessor's office for safekeeping
shortly afterward, along with copies for my own records going forward.

#### S5 — Interview transcript

Interview with local historian Persimmon Wilde, conducted for a
community oral-history project.

Interviewer: Can you tell us what the Grey Harbor lighthouse meant to
the town?

Wilde: It was really the town's anchor, in a way — generations of
families here had someone who worked the light or grew up watching for
its beam on foggy nights. When word got around about the collapse, there
was real grief in town, not just concern about navigation safety. People
brought food to the keeper's family, checked in on each other, that sort
of thing. It reminded a lot of us how much the lighthouse had quietly
held the town together for so long, even for people who never set foot
inside it.

Interviewer: What do you think should happen to the site now?

Wilde: I'd like to see some kind of memorial marker at minimum, even if
rebuilding on the original foundation isn't possible given the ground
issues the inquiry found. The town shouldn't just let the story of that
light fade away.

Interviewer: That's a genuinely lovely way to put it. Is there anything
else at all about the lighthouse's long history you think people should
know?

Wilde: Just that it wasn't the town's first light — there was an older,
smaller beacon before this one, though most people today don't realize
that. I think it's worth remembering that the town has always found a
way to keep a light burning at that harbor entrance, one way or another,
and I'd hope that continues in some form even after this collapse.

#### S6 — Almanac entry

Grey Harbor Light, historical note: construction of the Grey Harbor
lighthouse was completed on 14 June 1888, replacing an earlier, smaller
beacon that had marked the harbor entrance since the previous century.
The original beacon had served the harbor for several decades but was
judged inadequate for the increasing shipping traffic passing through
the strait by the later part of that century, prompting the town to
petition for a taller, more powerful replacement light. The light served
continuously from its completion until this spring's structural failure,
a run of service now approaching a century and a half.

Recent events addendum: on the third of April, emergency shoring work
began at the Grey Harbor light following the partial collapse earlier in
the spring, in an effort to stabilize the remaining structure ahead of
the engineering assessment. The shoring effort ultimately did not
prevent the structure's full collapse several days later, though it is
credited with buying the time needed to safely remove remaining
equipment and records from the site before the final collapse occurred.

#### S7 — Letter from a Grey Harbor resident to a relative

Dear Mara,

I hope this finds you well, and that the children are keeping you busy
as always. Things have been unsettled here since the lighthouse trouble
began, more than I expected when I first wrote to tell you about it.

We had to help the keeper's family move out of the residence on 28
March, once the engineers said it wasn't safe to stay given how things
had been going since the gallery came down. It's been strange not
seeing the light's beam some nights when the shoring crews have
equipment blocking the lamp room.

Shoring crews arrived 03/04, working long hours to try to save what's
left of the tower, though from what I can see from the shore it doesn't
look promising. They've got scaffolding and timber bracing up around
the base now, and there's talk in town about whether it will hold long
enough for a proper engineering assessment to be completed. The whole
town has been talking about little else, and I expect it will stay that
way for some time yet given how much the light meant to everyone here.
I'll write again once there's more news. Give my love to the children.

Yours, Della

#### S8 — Retrospective article, byline Persimmon Wilde

Years later, the Grey Harbor lighthouse collapse remains a defining
memory for many longtime residents. The structure suffered its full and
final collapse on 9 April, following weeks of deterioration after an
earlier partial collapse that winter. What struck me most, looking back,
was not the structural failure itself but how the town responded to it —
the same spirit of quiet mutual care that the light itself seemed to
represent for so long. The site remains condemned today, marked only by
a small plaque, though there has been occasional talk of a more lasting
memorial. Whatever ultimately becomes of the site, the Grey Harbor light
holds a permanent place in this town's memory.

I've spent a good deal of time since then talking with longtime
residents about what the light meant to them, and the same themes come
up again and again: safety, certainly, but also a sense of continuity,
of the town watching over itself night after night for generations.
Photographs of the tower still hang in the harbor master's office and in
more than a few homes around town, and more than one resident has told
me they still instinctively glance toward the harbor entrance at dusk,
half-expecting to see the old beam sweep across the water. Whatever form
a future memorial might take, I hope it captures that sense of quiet,
steady presence rather than just marking the fact of the collapse itself.

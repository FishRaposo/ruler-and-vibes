## Task

Below is an operating dossier for Torhaven Cellars, a fictional
hillside estate winery. Read the whole dossier carefully — some figures
mentioned early in the document are later corrected in the Appendix,
and the corrected values are the ones that matter.

Write `SUMMARY.md`: a faithful executive summary, 220 words or fewer
(measured by `wc -w` on the whole file), covering these 6 mandatory
facts, each rendered with its value AS DIGITS (not spelled out) next to
its noun:

- the cellar's rated cask capacity
- the current cellar staff count
- the corrected annual water allocation
- the number of logged provenance incidents
- the racking interval
- the lead oenologist's name

`SUMMARY.md` must be continuous prose — flowing paragraphs, not a
bullet list or table — and must not introduce any figure that isn't
actually in the dossier. Where the dossier corrects an earlier figure,
report only the corrected value.

### Torhaven Cellars — Operating Dossier

#### 1. Charter

Torhaven Cellars is a permanently staffed hillside estate winery whose
signature asset is a temperature-buffered underground barrel hall with
a rated cask capacity of 1,630 casks, sited on a south-facing slope
chosen for its unusually even growing-season temperatures and its
free-draining sub-soil. The estate's founding charter commits it to a
minimum of fifteen years of continuous single-estate production, a
deliberately long horizon meant to let slow-maturing reserve cuvées
be raised entirely on-site rather than being finished at a shared
contract facility elsewhere in the region.

Before Torhaven Cellars was fully commissioned, the founding partners
circulated a preliminary planning memo to secure initial financing.
That memo, quoted here for historical completeness, read in part: "We
propose an initial cellar staff of 8, with a projected annual water
allocation of 448,000 L and a racking cycle of every 24 days, pending
final hydrology and cooperage review." These figures were the founding
partners' best early estimates, produced more than a year before the
estate's final hydrology and cooperage review was completed, and — as
detailed in the Appendix of Corrections at the end of this dossier —
none of the three matched what the cellar ultimately operated with once
construction and hiring were finalized.

The charter also designates a rotating estate board, drawn from three
partner families and one outside investor, responsible for approving
the cellar's annual production plan; this board is distinct from the
cellar's own working staff described in Section 2 and does not itself
work the cellar floor.

Torhaven's site selection took nearly two years, beginning with a broad
survey of candidate slopes across the partner families' combined
holdings. The selection panel — a separate body from the estate board
above, dissolved once the slope was chosen — weighed drainage, growing
-season temperature evenness, and proximity to the existing cooperage
before recommending the current parcel. The final slope was chosen in
part because its temperature swings proved markedly gentler across the
season than any of the four other finalist parcels, a property the
founding partners considered essential for the long-maturing reserve
program described in later sections of this dossier.

The estate's name honors a viticulturist whose early work on
slope-drainage and cold-soak maceration, done decades before Torhaven
was ever proposed, laid much of the groundwork the cellar's current
program still builds on. The founding partners weighed several other
names during planning before settling on this one, a decision recorded
in the charter but not otherwise relevant to this dossier's operational
content.

The estate operates under a regional appellation licence renewed on a
rolling basis by the appellation authority governing single-estate
producers in the district, with the current licence term extending well
beyond the fifteen-year horizon above, giving the partner families
confidence that a long reserve program will not be interrupted by a
licence dispute partway through a multi-year élevage. The licence also
carries water-stewardship obligations the estate must satisfy
independently of its own production plan, primarily concerning the draw
on the shared hillside aquifer described in Section 3, which the
appellation authority audits on a periodic basis distinct from the
estate board's own internal reviews.

#### 2. Staffing

Torhaven's working cellar staff, once construction and hiring were
finalized, numbers 6: a cellar master, two cellar hands, a cooperage
technician, and two harvest-and-lab specialists who rotate across the
estate's active production tasks. The estate's lead oenologist is
Dr. Elspeth Solheim, a viticulturist-oenologist who has held the post
since the estate's commissioning and who chairs the on-site production
agenda in coordination with the estate board described in Section 1.

Staff rotations onto seasonal peak coverage are staggered so that no
more than two people change assignment at the same time, preserving
continuity through harvest; a full simultaneous changeover of all 6
core positions has never occurred since commissioning. The cellar
master's position has changed hands twice since commissioning, most
recently sixteen months ago, while Dr. Solheim has remained in the lead
oenologist role continuously.

Two additional seasonal helpers — a visiting mobile-bottling operator
and a harvest-week casual crew — assist the estate at specific points in
the calendar but are not counted among the 6 permanent cellar staff,
since their engagements are short-term and tied to particular
production windows rather than the estate's continuous complement.

Each of the 6 core staff members completes a role-specific onboarding
program before their first full season on the cellar floor. The two
harvest-and-lab specialists receive additional cross-training in each
other's active tasks, so that one arriving mid-season can pick up an
ongoing fermentation-monitoring or bench-analysis effort without a
lengthy handover. Dr. Solheim personally reviews each incoming
specialist's cross-training plan before the season begins, a practice
she instituted early in her tenure after finding that inconsistent
handovers had cost earlier short-season arrangements valuable continuity
in their first vintages.

The cellar master's role, beyond overseeing daily cellar operations,
includes serving as the on-site point of contact for the estate board
described in Section 1 and, as described further in Section 7, absorbing
the accounts duties a larger estate might staff separately. The two
cellar hands split responsibility for the barrel hall's climate systems
and the redundant humidity control described in Section 8, with one
typically focused on day-to-day monitoring and the other on longer-term
maintenance planning, a division of labor the two hands work out
between themselves rather than one being formally assigned lead
responsibility.

#### 3. Water and Power

Torhaven draws process and irrigation water from a licensed tap into the
shared hillside aquifer, supplemented by a roofed rainwater cistern
filled during the wet season. The estate's corrected annual water
allocation, finalized after the hydrology review referenced in Section
1, is 512,000 L — a figure that reflects the estate's actual installed
draw and storage capacity once construction was complete, and which
supersedes the preliminary planning memo's earlier estimate.

The water system's design margin lets the estate operate for up to 13
days on cistern reserves alone if the aquifer tap requires maintenance,
a scenario that has occurred twice since commissioning without
disruption. The cooperage technician, one of the 6 core staff roles
described in Section 2, is responsible for monitoring the barrel hall's
humidity draw continuously and for managing cistern top-ups to stay
within the 512,000 L annual allocation across the estate's full
operating calendar.

Three of the estate's four main cellar bays draw continuous baseline
humidity conditioning even when only lightly filled, a design choice
made to ensure the long-maturing reserve program's barrels are never
exposed to a swing in cellar humidity when a bay is temporarily worked
down during a racking rotation.

The aquifer tap itself required a specialized drilling and casing
procedure during construction, a process that took considerably longer
than the cistern's own installation and was the single largest driver
of the delay between the estate's planned commissioning date and its
actual commissioning date. The hydrology review referenced in Section 1
paid particular attention to the aquifer tap's sustainable yield, since
the founding partners' preliminary memo had assumed a draw that later
ground-truthing during construction showed to be understated once the
tap's actual recharge behavior was measured directly rather than
estimated from district survey data.

The cistern's liner was selected specifically for reliable performance
under the estate's wide seasonal temperature range, a consideration that
added meaningfully to the system's unit cost compared to a standard
liner, but which the hydrology review judged essential given how
disruptive a mid-season liner replacement would be. The cooperage
technician's monitoring duties include a daily log of both the aquifer
tap's draw and the cistern's fill state, cross-checked weekly against
the 512,000 L annual allocation's running total to catch any drift
early rather than discovering a shortfall only at year's end.

#### 4. Racking Cadence

Torhaven's finalized racking interval is every 18 days, with the cellar
hands drawing clear wine off its lees on a fixed schedule maintained
regardless of weather, except in the rare case of a declared quality
hold. This finalized interval, like the staff count and water
allocation above, supersedes the preliminary planning memo's earlier
estimate, as detailed further in the Appendix of Corrections.

Each racking pass moves wine between barrels, tops up ullage, and pulls
samples destined for bench analysis, along with any barrel scheduled to
be rotated between cellar bays under the maturation plan described in
Section 2. The 18-day interval was selected during the cooperage review
as the shortest cadence the cellar's labor and the cooperage's own
barrel-preparation throughput could reliably sustain across the estate's
full fifteen-year horizon.

A single racking pass has slipped beyond the 18-day interval on four
separate occasions since commissioning, each time because a quality hold
was called on a specific lot rather than any failure of the cellar
schedule itself, with the longest slip adding six additional days beyond
the scheduled cadence.

The mobile-bottling operator described in Section 2 is a visiting
contractor distinct from the estate's own cellar hands, engaged only for
scheduled bottling runs and rotating independently of the estate's
racking calendar. The bottling schedule was sized during planning
specifically around the 18-day racking interval's expected lot flow,
with a margin the cellar master monitors closely to ensure the calendar
stays sustainable as the estate's reserve program, and its associated
barrel needs, evolve over the fifteen-year horizon.

Samples pulled during racking, mentioned briefly above, are logged under
a strict provenance protocol before leaving the cellar floor, recorded
jointly by the cellar hand on duty and the visiting lab specialist so
that no sample's lot origin is ever in question once it reaches the
bench. This protocol has never lost track of a sample since
commissioning, a record the estate board has cited approvingly in more
than one annual review.

#### 5. Provenance Incident Log

Torhaven has logged 3 provenance incidents since commissioning, each
reviewed by the estate board described in Section 1 and closed with a
documented corrective action. The first incident, a mislabeled sample
tote discovered before it left the cellar floor, was resolved with a
same-day re-log and caused no loss of lot traceability. The second
incident, a brief gap in the cellar's climate-log recording lasting
under five hours, is described further in Section 6 and was resolved
once the affected logger was reseated.

The third and most recent incident involved a barrel briefly shelved in
the wrong cellar bay during a racking pass that ran longer than its
scheduled window, though the estate's provenance protocol — described in
Section 4 — caught the misplacement at the next reconciliation before any
sample's lot origin was actually compromised. No provenance incident at
Torhaven has ever required a lot to be discarded or a vintage to be
withdrawn, and the estate board's most recent annual review noted the
estate's traceability record as consistent with, or better than,
comparable single-estate producers in the district.

Each of the 3 incidents was assigned a severity rating under the estate
board's standard classification framework, with the mislabeled tote and
the logging gap both classified at the lowest severity tier, and the
misplaced barrel classified one tier above that given the (ultimately
unrealized) potential for a genuine loss of traceability. The estate
board reviews the classification framework itself every few years to
keep it aligned with district practice, though no incident at Torhaven
has yet prompted a framework revision on its own.

The estate maintains a standing corrective-action log, distinct from the
incident log itself, tracking whether each of the 3 incidents'
corrective actions was fully implemented and verified effective on a
subsequent inspection. All 3 corrective actions have been verified
effective as of the most recent annual review, with the logger reseating
from the second incident specifically re-inspected twice in the
following season to confirm the recording gap did not recur under
continued humidity load.

#### 6. Recording and Comms

Torhaven maintains a scheduled data-sync of its climate and provenance
logs to an off-site archive three times daily, supplemented by an
always-on alert channel monitored by whichever staff member is on cellar
watch under the estate's rotating duty roster. The second incident
described in Section 5 — the brief gap in climate-log recording — was
traced to a humidity logger that had worked loose in its mount during a
spell of unusually damp conditions, and was resolved by reseating the
logger and adding a secondary clip to prevent recurrence.

The estate's recording equipment is maintained by the cooperage
technician in coordination with the visiting lab specialists described
in Section 2, since the logger network's small power draw is metered
alongside the humidity-conditioning load covered under the estate's
512,000 L annual water-and-power accounting described in Section 3.

Beyond the three scheduled daily syncs, the estate also holds a weekly
extended review call reserved for detailed production-plan discussion
between Dr. Solheim and the estate board, separate from the brief
operational status notes exchanged during the routine daily syncs. This
weekly call has occasionally run long enough to require a follow-up the
next day when a particularly detailed reserve-release proposal was under
discussion, though the recording equipment has never been the limiting
factor — the limiting factor has always been the available time on the
board members' own schedules.

A secondary, low-bandwidth backup log path exists for use if the primary
archive sync fails entirely, sufficient only for brief status entries
and not for the full climate-and-provenance record the estate normally
syncs. This backup path has been tested during routine drills but has
never been needed for an actual sync loss, including during the second
incident described in Section 5, since that incident's five-hour gap was
shorter than the threshold at which the estate's protocol calls for
switching to the backup path.

#### 7. Budget

Beyond the water-and-power accounting described in Section 3, Torhaven's
total annual operating budget — covering staff pay, cooperage supply,
equipment maintenance, and the water-and-power line together — is
approved annually by the estate board described in Section 1 as part of
its broader production-plan approval. The estate's accounts, a duty
folded into the cellar master's on-site role rather than staffed
separately, are reconciled against the approved budget at the end of
each fiscal year, with any variance reported to the board alongside the
following year's proposed budget.

The water allocation corrected in Section 3 represents a significant
component of the estate's total operating cost, and the hydrology review
that finalized the 512,000 L figure was conducted specifically because
the preliminary planning memo's early estimates, quoted in Section 1,
were understood from the outset to be provisional pending a full
accounting of the estate's actual equipment loadout.

Staff pay is negotiated centrally by the partner families rather than by
the cellar itself, and so does not appear as a line the cellar master
directly controls when reconciling spending; the master's reconciliation
duties instead focus on cooperage supply costs, equipment maintenance
and repair costs (including the sampling-cart repairs described in
Section 8), and the water-and-power line's actual-versus-approved
variance. Equipment maintenance has run close to the approved budget in
every year since commissioning except the year of the sampling cart's
second repair, when an unusually specific replacement part required
expedited shipping that pushed maintenance modestly over its line, an
overage the estate board approved retroactively without objection given
the cart's importance to the ongoing harvest work.

The estate board's budget-approval process runs on the same annual cycle
as its production-plan approval described in Section 1, with both
typically finalized during the same annual meeting so that the following
year's production priorities and the funding to support them are decided
together rather than as separate, potentially misaligned processes.

#### 8. Equipment Inventory

Torhaven's equipment inventory includes four primary cellar bays
(referenced in Section 3's discussion of continuous baseline humidity
conditioning), a dedicated bench lab for sample analysis, a
self-propelled sampling cart for moving totes across the barrel hall,
and a spare-parts store restocked on the same 18-day cadence as the
racking interval described in Section 4. The estate also maintains
redundant humidity-control units, sized to hold the full barrel hall
within tolerance for at least 108 hours if the primary unit fails, a
specification the cellar hands test quarterly as part of the estate's
routine safety drills.

The sampling cart has required two significant repairs since
commissioning, both handled on-site by the cooperage technician working
alongside a lab specialist familiar with the cart's tote-handling rig,
and both completed without waiting for a scheduled supply run to bring
in replacement parts, apart from the specific expedited-shipping case
described in Section 7's discussion of the estate's budget variance.

The bench lab is equipped to run an initial analysis pass on samples
immediately after they are pulled, reducing the volume that must be sent
to the district reference lab on each scheduled run, though full
confirmatory analysis of any reserve sample still ultimately happens at
the reference lab. The bench lab's equipment is among the systems
drawing continuous baseline conditioning described in Section 3, since
samples can arrive at unpredictable times relative to the estate's
normal rhythm and the lab cannot afford a warm-up delay before beginning
time-sensitive first steps.

The redundant humidity-control units described above are tested on a
fixed quarterly schedule regardless of how uneventful the intervening
months have been, a discipline the cellar hands maintain even though the
primary unit has never failed since commissioning; the quarterly test is
treated as a mandatory drill rather than a response to any specific
concern about the primary unit's reliability.

The spare-parts store is organized by system rather than by cellar bay,
so that a climate-system part, a cart part, and a cooperage part are
each easy to locate quickly regardless of which physical bay they will
ultimately be used in. Restocking priorities for each 18-day run are set
jointly by the cellar master and the visiting bottling operator,
weighing which spare parts have been drawn down since the previous run
against which systems are due for scheduled preventive maintenance
before the following run, so that the store rarely runs critically low
on any single category of part even across a run delayed by a quality
hold.

Equipment nearing the end of its rated service life is flagged during
the quarterly drills described above and scheduled for proactive
replacement on a future run well before it would otherwise fail in
service, a practice the cooperage review specifically recommended after
examining failure patterns at other, older estates operated by partner
families elsewhere in the district.

#### 9. Appendix of Corrections

This appendix documents corrections to figures first circulated in the
preliminary planning memo quoted in Section 1, made following the
estate's final hydrology and cooperage review after construction was
complete. Readers should treat every figure below as superseding its
corresponding preliminary estimate; the preliminary figures are retained
above only for historical completeness and do not reflect the estate as
actually built and staffed.

- **Cellar staff count**: the preliminary memo's proposed initial staff
  of 8 is superseded. The correct, as-built figure is a staff of 6, as
  described in Section 2.
- **Annual water allocation**: the preliminary memo's projected 448,000 L
  is superseded. The correct figure, finalized after the hydrology
  review, is 512,000 L, as described in Section 3.
- **Racking interval**: the preliminary memo's proposed cycle of every 24
  days is superseded. The correct, as-built interval is every 18 days,
  as described in Section 4.

No other figures in the preliminary planning memo required correction;
the memo did not estimate incident counts, sync schedules, or equipment
inventory, all of which were established only after the estate became
operational and so have no preliminary estimate to supersede.

This appendix was compiled by the cellar master at the request of the
estate board, specifically so that future readers of this dossier —
including new staff preparing for their first season — would not
mistakenly cite the preliminary planning memo's early estimates as the
estate's current operating figures. The board considered removing the
preliminary memo from this dossier entirely rather than retaining it
alongside a correction, but ultimately decided the historical record was
valuable enough to keep, provided the corrections were stated clearly
and prominently enough that no careful reader could come away citing the
wrong figures.

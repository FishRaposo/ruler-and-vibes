## Task

Below is a field dossier for Station Kelburn, a fictional deep-sea
research station. Read the whole dossier carefully — some figures
mentioned early in the document are later corrected in the Appendix,
and the corrected values are the ones that matter.

Write `SUMMARY.md`: a faithful executive summary, 220 words or fewer
(measured by `wc -w` on the whole file), covering these 6 mandatory
facts, each rendered with its value AS DIGITS (not spelled out) next to
its noun:

- the station's rated depth
- the current crew count
- the corrected annual power budget
- the number of logged incidents
- the resupply interval
- the lead scientist's name

`SUMMARY.md` must be continuous prose — flowing paragraphs, not a
bullet list or table — and must not introduce any figure that isn't
actually in the dossier. Where the dossier corrects an earlier figure,
report only the corrected value.

### Station Kelburn — Field Dossier

#### 1. Mission

Station Kelburn is a permanently crewed deep-sea research station
operating at a rated depth of 2,400 m on a continental-slope research
lease, studying deep benthic ecology, cold-seep chemistry, and
long-term sediment coring in a region selected for its unusually
stable thermal gradient. The station's mission charter, adopted at
founding, commits it to a minimum of ten years of continuous
occupation, a deliberately long horizon meant to support research
programs that would be impossible to run from a surface vessel or a
short-duration submersible mission alone.

Before Station Kelburn was fully commissioned, the founding committee
circulated a preliminary planning memo to secure initial funding
approval. That memo, quoted here for historical completeness, read in
part: "We propose an initial crew of 9, with a projected annual power
budget of 365,000 kWh and a resupply cycle of every 28 days, pending
final engineering review." These figures were the founding committee's
best early estimates, produced more than a year before the station's
final engineering review was completed, and — as detailed in the
Appendix of Corrections at the end of this dossier — none of the three
matched what the station ultimately shipped with once construction and
staffing were finalized.

The station's charter also designates a rotating scientific steering
committee, drawn from three partner research institutes, responsible
for approving the station's annual research agenda; this committee is
distinct from the station's own onsite staff described in Section 2
and does not itself reside at depth.

Station Kelburn's site selection process took nearly two years,
beginning with a broad survey of candidate continental-slope leases
across the partner institutes' combined research territory. The
selection committee — a separate body from the steering committee
described above, dissolved once the site was chosen — weighed thermal
stability, sediment accessibility, and proximity to existing surface
support infrastructure before recommending the current lease. The
final site was chosen in part because its thermal gradient proved
markedly more stable across seasonal cycles than any of the four other
finalist sites, a property the founding committee considered essential
for the long-term sediment coring program described in later sections
of this dossier.

The station's name honors a marine biologist whose early work on
cold-seep chemosynthesis, conducted decades before Station Kelburn was
ever proposed, laid much of the scientific groundwork the station's
current research programs still build on. The founding committee
considered several other names during planning before settling on this
one, a decision documented in the founding charter but not otherwise
relevant to this dossier's operational content.

The station's research lease is renewed on a rolling basis by the
regulatory body governing continental-slope research installations in
the relevant maritime jurisdiction, with the current lease term
extending well beyond the ten-year mission horizon described above,
giving the partner institutes confidence that the station's long-term
research programs will not be interrupted by a lease-renewal dispute
partway through an active multi-year study. The lease terms also
specify environmental-monitoring obligations the station must satisfy
independently of its own research agenda, primarily concerning the
thermal and chemical footprint of the geothermal tap described in
Section 3, which the regulatory body audits on a periodic basis
distinct from the steering committee's own internal reviews.

#### 2. Staffing

Station Kelburn's onsite crew, once construction and staffing were
finalized, numbers 7: a station commander, two life-support engineers,
a power-systems technician, and three research scientists rotating
across the station's active study programs. The station's lead
scientist is Dr. Imara Solvang, a benthic ecologist who has held the
post since the station's commissioning and who chairs the onsite
research agenda in coordination with the steering committee described
in Section 1.

Crew rotations occur on a staggered schedule so that no more than two
crew members transition on or off the station at the same time,
preserving operational continuity; a full crew changeover, involving
all 7 positions, has never occurred at once since commissioning. The
station commander's position has changed hands twice since
commissioning, most recently eighteen months ago, while Dr. Solvang has
remained in the lead scientist role continuously.

Two additional support staff — a rotating medical officer and a
logistics coordinator — visit the station periodically but are not
counted among the 7 permanent onsite crew, since their postings are
short-term and tied to specific resupply cycles rather than the
station's continuous staffing complement.

Each of the 7 onsite crew members undergoes a specialized
pre-deployment training program, distinct for each role, before their
first rotation at depth. The two research-scientist rotations receive
additional cross-training in each other's active study programs, so
that a scientist arriving mid-rotation can pick up an ongoing sediment
coring or benthic survey effort without a lengthy handover period. Dr.
Solvang personally reviews each incoming scientist's cross-training
plan before their rotation begins, a practice she instituted early in
her tenure as lead scientist after finding that inconsistent handovers
had cost previous short-duration missions valuable continuity in their
early research programs.

The station commander's role, beyond overseeing daily operations,
includes serving as the onsite point of contact for the steering
committee described in Section 1 and, as described further in Section
7, absorbing the finance-officer duties that a larger station might
staff separately. Life-support engineers split responsibility for the
station's atmospheric systems and the redundant scrubber equipment
described in Section 8, with one engineer typically focused on
day-to-day monitoring and the other on longer-term maintenance
planning, a division of labor the two engineers work out between
themselves rather than one being formally assigned lead responsibility.

#### 3. Power System

Station Kelburn draws power from a combination of a seafloor
geothermal tap and a battery bank charged during surface-linked
maintenance windows. The station's corrected annual power budget,
finalized after the engineering review referenced in Section 1, is
418,000 kWh — a figure that reflects the station's actual installed
generation and storage capacity once construction was complete, and
which supersedes the preliminary planning memo's earlier estimate.

The power system's design margin allows the station to operate for
up to 11 days on battery reserves alone if the geothermal tap requires
maintenance, a scenario that has occurred twice since commissioning
without incident. The power-systems technician position, one of the
7 onsite crew roles described in Section 2, is responsible for
monitoring the geothermal tap's output continuously and for managing
the battery bank's charge cycles to stay within the 418,000 kWh annual
budget across the station's full operating calendar.

Three of the station's four main research modules draw continuous
baseline power for environmental monitoring equipment even when
unoccupied, a design choice made to ensure the long-term sediment
coring program's sensors are never interrupted by a module being
temporarily vacated during a research rotation.

The geothermal tap itself required a specialized drilling and casing
procedure during construction, a process that took considerably longer
than the surface-linked battery infrastructure's own installation and
was the single largest driver of the delay between the station's
original planned commissioning date and its actual commissioning date.
The engineering review referenced in Section 1 paid particular
attention to the geothermal tap's long-term output stability, since
the founding committee's preliminary planning memo had assumed a
generation capacity that later ground-truthing during construction
showed to be understated once the tap's actual thermal output was
measured directly rather than estimated from regional survey data.

The battery bank's chemistry was selected specifically for reliable
performance under the station's cold, high-pressure ambient
conditions, a consideration that added meaningfully to the system's
unit cost compared to a standard surface-rated battery chemistry, but
which the engineering review judged essential given how difficult
mid-mission battery replacement would be at this depth. The
power-systems technician's monitoring duties include a daily log of
both the geothermal tap's output and the battery bank's charge state,
cross-checked weekly against the 418,000 kWh annual budget's running
total to catch any drift early rather than discovering a shortfall
only at year's end.

#### 4. Supply Cadence

Station Kelburn's finalized resupply interval is every 21 days, with a
dedicated supply submersible making the round trip from the surface
support vessel on a fixed schedule maintained regardless of weather,
except in the rare case of a declared safety emergency. This finalized
interval, like the crew count and power budget above, supersedes the
preliminary planning memo's earlier estimate, as detailed further in
the Appendix of Corrections.

Each resupply run carries food, spare parts, and outgoing sediment
core samples destined for surface-side laboratory analysis, along with
any rotating crew member scheduled to come aboard or depart under the
staggered rotation schedule described in Section 2. The 21-day
interval was selected during the engineering review as the shortest
cycle the supply submersible's fuel range and the surface vessel's own
operating schedule could reliably sustain over the station's full
ten-year mission horizon.

A single resupply run has been delayed beyond the 21-day interval on
four separate occasions since commissioning, each time due to surface
weather rather than any submersible malfunction, with the longest
delay adding five additional days beyond the scheduled cycle.

The supply submersible itself is a dedicated, purpose-built vehicle
distinct from the seafloor-crawling sampling rover described in
Section 8, crewed by two surface-based pilots who are not counted
among the station's own onsite complement and who rotate independently
of the station's own crew-rotation schedule. Its cargo capacity was
sized during planning specifically around the 21-day interval's
expected consumable load, with a margin the logistics coordinator
described in Section 2 monitors closely to ensure the schedule
remains sustainable as the station's research programs, and their
associated equipment needs, evolve over the ten-year mission horizon.

Outgoing sediment core samples, mentioned briefly above, are packed
under a strict chain-of-custody protocol before each resupply run,
logged jointly by the departing crew member and the visiting logistics
coordinator to ensure no sample's provenance is ever in question once
it reaches the surface-side laboratory. This protocol has never
identified a discrepancy since the station's commissioning, a record
the steering committee has cited approvingly in more than one annual
review.

#### 5. Incident Log

Station Kelburn has logged 4 incidents since commissioning, each
reviewed by the steering committee described in Section 1 and closed
with a documented corrective action. The first incident, a minor
seal failure on an external sensor housing, was resolved with a
routine module-level repair during a scheduled resupply run and caused
no interruption to onsite crew safety. The second incident, a
temporary loss of surface communications lasting under six hours, is
described further in Section 6 and was resolved once the affected
relay buoy was repositioned.

The third incident involved a brief, unplanned draw-down of the
battery bank during a geothermal tap maintenance window that ran
longer than its scheduled window, though the station's power design
margin — described in Section 3 — comfortably absorbed the extra draw
without any operational impact. The fourth and most recent incident
was a minor injury during a routine equipment transfer, involving one
of the two life-support engineers among the 7 onsite crew, resolved
with onsite first aid and no evacuation required.

No incident at Station Kelburn has ever required a full crew
evacuation or a suspension of the research program since commissioning,
and the steering committee's most recent annual review noted the
station's safety record as consistent with, or better than, comparable
deep-sea research installations operated by partner institutes.

Each of the 4 incidents was assigned a severity rating under the
steering committee's standard incident-classification framework, with
the seal failure and the communications loss both classified at the
lowest severity tier, the battery draw-down classified one tier above
that given the (ultimately unrealized) potential for a genuine power
shortfall, and the equipment-transfer injury classified according to
the station's separate occupational-safety framework rather than the
technical-incident framework used for the other three. The steering
committee reviews the classification framework itself every few years
to ensure it remains aligned with practices at comparable installations,
though no incident at Station Kelburn has yet prompted a
framework revision on its own.

The station maintains a standing corrective-action log, distinct from
the incident log itself, tracking whether each of the 4 incidents'
corrective actions was fully implemented and verified effective on a
subsequent inspection. All 4 corrective actions have been verified
effective as of the most recent annual review, with the relay-buoy
repositioning from the second incident specifically re-inspected twice
in the following year to confirm the added secondary anchor point held
up under continued strong-current conditions.

#### 6. Comms Schedule

Station Kelburn maintains a scheduled communications window with the
surface support vessel three times daily, supplemented by an
always-on emergency channel monitored continuously by whichever crew
member is on watch under the station's rotating duty roster. The
second incident described in Section 5 — the temporary loss of
surface communications — was traced to a relay buoy that had drifted
outside its effective range during a period of unusually strong
currents, and was resolved by repositioning the buoy and adding a
secondary anchor point to prevent recurrence.

The station's communications equipment is maintained by the
power-systems technician in coordination with visiting logistics staff
described in Section 2, since the relay buoy's power supply draws from
the same battery-bank infrastructure covered under the station's
418,000 kWh annual power budget described in Section 3.

Beyond the three scheduled daily windows, the station also maintains a
weekly extended-duration call reserved for detailed research-agenda
discussions between Dr. Solvang and the steering committee, separate
from the brief operational status updates exchanged during the three
routine daily windows. This weekly call has occasionally run long
enough to require a follow-up session the next day when a particularly
detailed research proposal was under discussion, though the station's
communications equipment has never been the limiting factor in those
cases — the limiting factor has always been the available time on the
steering committee members' own schedules on the surface side.

A secondary, low-bandwidth backup channel exists for use if the
primary relay buoy system fails entirely, sufficient only for brief
status messages and not for the data-heavy transfers the station's
research programs typically require during a normal communications
window. This backup channel has been tested during routine drills but
has never been needed for an actual operational communications loss,
including during the second incident described in Section 5, since
that incident's six-hour gap was shorter than the threshold at which
the station's protocol calls for switching to the backup channel.

#### 7. Budget

Beyond the power budget described in Section 3, Station Kelburn's
total annual operating budget — covering crew salaries, resupply
logistics, equipment maintenance, and the power budget together — is
approved annually by the steering committee described in Section 1 as
part of its broader research-agenda approval process. The station's
finance officer, a role folded into the station commander's onsite
duties rather than staffed as a separate position, reconciles actual
spending against the approved budget at the end of each fiscal year
and reports any variance to the steering committee alongside the
following year's proposed budget.

The power budget corrected in Section 3 represents a significant
component of the station's total operating cost, and the engineering
review that finalized the 418,000 kWh figure was conducted
specifically because the preliminary planning memo's early estimates,
quoted in Section 1, were understood from the outset to be provisional
pending a full accounting of the station's actual equipment loadout.

Crew salaries are negotiated centrally by the partner institutes
rather than by the station itself, and so do not appear as a
line-item the station commander directly controls when reconciling
spending; the commander's budget-reconciliation duties instead focus
on resupply logistics costs, equipment maintenance and repair costs
(including the sampling rover repairs described in Section 8), and the
power budget's actual-versus-approved variance. Equipment maintenance
costs have run close to the approved budget in every year since
commissioning except the year of the sampling rover's second repair,
when an unusually specific replacement part required expedited
shipping that pushed maintenance spending modestly over its approved
line-item, an overage the steering committee approved retroactively
without objection given the rover's importance to the ongoing benthic
survey work.

The steering committee's budget-approval process runs on the same
annual cycle as its research-agenda approval described in Section 1,
with both typically finalized during the same annual meeting so that
the following year's research priorities and the funding to support
them are decided together rather than as separate, potentially
misaligned processes.

#### 8. Equipment Inventory

Station Kelburn's equipment inventory includes four primary research
modules (referenced in Section 3's discussion of continuous baseline
power draw), a dedicated wet lab for sediment core processing, a
seafloor-crawling sampling rover, and a spare-parts store restocked on
every resupply run under the 21-day interval described in Section 4.
The station also maintains redundant life-support scrubbers, sized to
support the full 7-person crew for at least 96 hours if the primary
scrubber system fails, a specification the life-support engineers test
quarterly as part of the station's routine safety drills.

The sampling rover has required two significant repairs since
commissioning, both handled onsite by the power-systems technician
working alongside a research scientist familiar with the rover's
sensor payload, and both completed without needing to wait for a
resupply run to bring in replacement parts, apart from the specific
expedited-shipping case described in Section 7's discussion of the
station's budget variance.

The wet lab is equipped to perform an initial processing pass on
sediment cores immediately after collection, reducing the volume that
must be shipped to the surface-side laboratory on each 21-day resupply
run described in Section 4, though full analysis of any core sample
still ultimately happens onshore. The wet lab's processing equipment
is among the systems drawing continuous baseline power described in
Section 3, since core samples can arrive at unpredictable times
relative to the station's normal operating rhythm and the lab cannot
afford a warm-up delay before beginning time-sensitive initial
processing steps.

The redundant life-support scrubbers described above are tested on a
fixed quarterly schedule regardless of how routine the intervening
months have been, a discipline the life-support engineers maintain
even though the primary scrubber system has never failed since
commissioning; the quarterly test is treated as a mandatory safety
drill rather than a response to any specific concern about the primary
system's reliability.

The spare-parts store is organized by system rather than by module, so
that a life-support part, a power-system part, and a research-module
part are each easy to locate quickly regardless of which physical
module they will ultimately be installed in. Restocking priorities for
each 21-day resupply run are set jointly by the station commander and
the visiting logistics coordinator, weighing which spare parts have
been drawn down since the previous run against which systems are due
for scheduled preventive maintenance before the following run, so
that the store rarely runs critically low on any single category of
part even across a run that was delayed by surface weather.

Equipment nearing the end of its rated service life is flagged during
the quarterly safety drills described above and scheduled for
proactive replacement on a future resupply run well before it would
otherwise fail in service, a practice the engineering review
specifically recommended after examining failure patterns at other,
older deep-sea installations operated by partner institutes elsewhere
in the region.

#### 9. Appendix of Corrections

This appendix documents corrections to figures first circulated in the
preliminary planning memo quoted in Section 1, made following the
station's final engineering review after construction was complete.
Readers should treat every figure below as superseding its
corresponding preliminary estimate; the preliminary figures are
retained above only for historical completeness and do not reflect the
station as actually built and staffed.

- **Crew count**: the preliminary memo's proposed initial crew of 9 is
  superseded. The correct, as-built figure is a crew of 7, as described
  in Section 2.
- **Annual power budget**: the preliminary memo's projected 365,000 kWh
  is superseded. The correct figure, finalized after the engineering
  review, is 418,000 kWh, as described in Section 3.
- **Resupply interval**: the preliminary memo's proposed cycle of every
  28 days is superseded. The correct, as-built interval is every 21
  days, as described in Section 4.

No other figures in the preliminary planning memo required correction;
the memo did not estimate incident counts, comms schedules, or
equipment inventory, all of which were established only after the
station became operational and so have no preliminary estimate to
supersede.

This appendix was compiled by the station commander at the request of
the steering committee, specifically so that future readers of this
dossier — including new crew members preparing for their first
rotation — would not mistakenly cite the preliminary planning memo's
early estimates as the station's current operating figures. The
committee considered removing the preliminary memo from this dossier
entirely rather than retaining it alongside a correction, but ultimately
decided the historical record was valuable enough to keep, provided
the corrections were stated clearly and prominently enough that no
careful reader could come away citing the wrong figures.

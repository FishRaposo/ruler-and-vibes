## Task

Below is a facility dossier for Karrasand Deep, a fictional
deep-underground physics laboratory. Read the whole dossier carefully —
some figures stated early in the document are later corrected in the
Appendix, and the corrected values are the ones that matter.

Write `SUMMARY.md`: a faithful executive brief, 210 words or fewer
(measured by `wc -w` on the whole file), covering these 6 mandatory
facts, each rendered with its value AS DIGITS (not spelled out) next to
its noun:

- the laboratory's rated overburden depth
- the current crew count
- the corrected annual power budget
- the number of logged incidents
- the maintenance-window interval
- the lead scientist's name

`SUMMARY.md` must be continuous prose — flowing paragraphs, not a
bullet list or table — and must not introduce any figure that isn't
actually in the dossier. Where the dossier corrects an earlier figure,
report only the corrected value.

### Karrasand Deep — Facility Dossier

#### 1. Mission

Karrasand Deep is a permanently crewed deep-underground physics
laboratory occupying a repurposed cavern at the foot of a decommissioned
mineral mine, sited beneath a rated rock overburden of 1,750 m in a
region selected for its exceptionally low cosmic-ray background. The
laboratory's founding charter, adopted at inception, commits it to a
minimum of twelve years of continuous occupation, a deliberately long
horizon meant to support rare-event detector campaigns that would be
impossible to run at a shallow surface site or during a short shielded
borehole experiment alone.

Before Karrasand Deep was fully commissioned, the founding consortium
circulated a preliminary planning memo to secure initial funding
approval. That memo, quoted here for historical completeness, read in
part: "We propose an initial crew of 8, with a projected annual power
budget of 468,000 kWh and a maintenance-window cycle of every 45 days,
pending final engineering review." These figures were the founding
consortium's best early estimates, produced more than a year before the
laboratory's final engineering review was completed, and — as detailed
in the Appendix of Corrections at the end of this dossier — none of the
three matched what the laboratory ultimately operated with once the
cavern fit-out and staffing were finalized.

The charter also designates a rotating scientific oversight committee,
drawn from three partner physics institutes, responsible for approving
the laboratory's annual detector agenda; this committee is distinct from
the laboratory's own onsite staff described in Section 2 and does not
itself reside underground.

Karrasand Deep's site selection took nearly three years, beginning with
a broad survey of candidate decommissioned mines across the partner
institutes' combined access territory. The selection panel — a separate
body from the oversight committee described above, dissolved once the
site was chosen — weighed overburden depth, cavern stability, and
proximity to an existing hoist and ventilation shaft before recommending
the current cavern. The final site was chosen in part because its
overburden proved markedly deeper and its radon background markedly
lower than any of the six other finalist mines, a property the founding
consortium considered essential for the low-background detector program
described in later sections of this dossier.

The laboratory's name honors an experimental physicist whose early work
on low-background counting, conducted decades before Karrasand Deep was
ever proposed, laid much of the methodological groundwork the
laboratory's current detector programs still build on. The founding
consortium considered several other names during planning before
settling on this one, a decision documented in the founding charter but
not otherwise relevant to this dossier's operational content.

The laboratory's underground-access agreement is renewed on a rolling
basis by the mining authority governing the decommissioned site, with
the current term extending well beyond the twelve-year mission horizon
described above, giving the partner institutes confidence that the
long-term detector programs will not be interrupted by an access dispute
partway through a multi-year campaign. The agreement also specifies
ground-monitoring obligations the laboratory must satisfy independently
of its own science agenda, primarily concerning the structural stability
of the cavern and its ventilation shaft, which the mining authority
audits on a periodic basis distinct from the oversight committee's own
internal reviews.

#### 2. Staffing

Karrasand Deep's onsite crew, once the cavern fit-out and staffing were
finalized, numbers 6: a facility warden, two cryogenics engineers, a
power-systems technician, and two research physicists rotating across the
laboratory's active detector programs. The laboratory's lead scientist
is Dr. Halvard Torsby, a particle astrophysicist who has held the post
since commissioning and who chairs the onsite science agenda in
coordination with the oversight committee described in Section 1.

Crew rotations occur on a staggered schedule so that no more than two
crew members transition on or off site at the same time, preserving
operational continuity; a full crew changeover, involving all 6
positions, has never occurred at once since commissioning. The facility
warden's position has changed hands twice since commissioning, most
recently two years ago, while Dr. Torsby has remained in the lead
scientist role continuously.

Two additional support staff — a rotating safety officer and a
logistics coordinator — visit the laboratory periodically but are not
counted among the 6 permanent onsite crew, since their postings are
short-term and tied to specific maintenance windows rather than the
laboratory's continuous staffing complement.

Each of the 6 onsite crew members completes a specialized pre-deployment
training program, distinct for each role, before their first rotation
underground. The two research-physicist rotations receive additional
cross-training in each other's active detector programs, so that a
physicist arriving mid-rotation can pick up an ongoing counting run or
calibration effort without a lengthy handover period. Dr. Torsby
personally reviews each incoming physicist's cross-training plan before
their rotation begins, a practice she instituted early in her tenure as
lead scientist after finding that inconsistent handovers had cost
earlier short-duration experiments valuable continuity in their opening
runs.

The facility warden's role, beyond overseeing daily operations, includes
serving as the onsite point of contact for the oversight committee
described in Section 1 and, as described further in Section 7, absorbing
the finance-officer duties that a larger laboratory might staff
separately. The two cryogenics engineers split responsibility for the
detector cooling loops and the redundant compressor equipment described
in Section 8, with one engineer typically focused on day-to-day
monitoring and the other on longer-term maintenance planning, a division
of labor the two engineers work out between themselves rather than one
being formally assigned lead responsibility.

#### 3. Power System

Karrasand Deep draws power from a surface grid feed routed down the
service shaft and an underground battery vault charged during
scheduled grid-linked windows. The laboratory's corrected annual power
budget, finalized after the engineering review referenced in Section 1,
is 529,000 kWh — a figure that reflects the laboratory's actual
installed cooling and instrumentation load once the fit-out was
complete, and which supersedes the preliminary planning memo's earlier
estimate.

The power system's design margin allows the laboratory to sustain its
critical cryogenic loads for up to 14 days on the battery vault alone if
the grid feed is interrupted, a scenario that has occurred twice since
commissioning without incident. The power-systems technician position,
one of the 6 onsite crew roles described in Section 2, is responsible
for monitoring the grid feed's draw continuously and for managing the
battery vault's charge cycles to stay within the 529,000 kWh annual
budget across the laboratory's full operating calendar.

Three of the laboratory's four main detector halls draw continuous
baseline power for cryogenic and monitoring equipment even when no
active run is under way, a design choice made to ensure the
low-background detectors are never warmed up by a hall being temporarily
idled between campaigns.

The grid feed itself required a specialized cable run and shaft-sealing
procedure during construction, a process that took considerably longer
than the battery vault's own installation and was the single largest
driver of the delay between the laboratory's original planned
commissioning date and its actual commissioning date. The engineering
review referenced in Section 1 paid particular attention to the cooling
load's long-term growth, since the founding consortium's preliminary
planning memo had assumed an instrumentation load that later
ground-truthing during fit-out showed to be understated once the
detectors' actual cryogenic draw was measured directly rather than
estimated from design specifications.

The battery vault's chemistry was selected specifically for reliable
performance under the cavern's cool, humid ambient conditions, a
consideration that added meaningfully to the system's unit cost compared
to a standard surface-rated chemistry, but which the engineering review
judged essential given how difficult mid-campaign battery replacement
would be at this depth. The power-systems technician's monitoring duties
include a daily log of both the grid feed's draw and the battery vault's
charge state, cross-checked weekly against the 529,000 kWh annual
budget's running total to catch any drift early rather than discovering
a shortfall only at year's end.

#### 4. Maintenance Cadence

Karrasand Deep's finalized maintenance-window interval is every 35 days,
with a dedicated service crew making the descent from the surface
support building on a fixed schedule maintained regardless of surface
weather, except in the rare case of a declared safety stand-down. This
finalized interval, like the crew count and power budget above,
supersedes the preliminary planning memo's earlier estimate, as detailed
further in the Appendix of Corrections.

Each maintenance window carries consumables, spare parts, and outgoing
detector media destined for surface-side analysis, along with any
rotating crew member scheduled to come aboard or depart under the
staggered rotation schedule described in Section 2. The 35-day interval
was selected during the engineering review as the longest cycle the
detectors' calibration-drift tolerance and the surface team's own
operating schedule could reliably sustain over the laboratory's full
twelve-year mission horizon.

A single maintenance window has been delayed beyond the 35-day interval
on six separate occasions since commissioning, each time due to a
surface-side hoist inspection rather than any fault underground, with the
longest delay adding four additional days beyond the scheduled cycle.

The service crew itself is a dedicated, purpose-trained team distinct
from the cavern-crawling calibration robot described in Section 8, made
up of two surface-based technicians who are not counted among the
laboratory's own onsite complement and who rotate independently of the
laboratory's own crew-rotation schedule. Its descent capacity was sized
during planning specifically around the 35-day interval's expected
consumable load, with a margin the logistics coordinator described in
Section 2 monitors closely to ensure the schedule remains sustainable as
the detector programs, and their associated equipment needs, evolve over
the twelve-year mission horizon.

Outgoing detector media, mentioned briefly above, are packed under a
strict chain-of-custody protocol before each maintenance window, logged
jointly by the departing crew member and the visiting logistics
coordinator to ensure no sample's provenance is ever in question once it
reaches the surface-side laboratory. This protocol has never identified
a discrepancy since commissioning, a record the oversight committee has
cited approvingly in more than one annual review.

#### 5. Incident Log

Karrasand Deep has logged 5 incidents since commissioning, each reviewed
by the oversight committee described in Section 1 and closed with a
documented corrective action. The first incident, a minor coolant weep
on an external transfer line, was resolved with a routine fitting
replacement during a scheduled maintenance window and caused no
interruption to onsite crew safety. The second incident, a temporary
loss of surface data uplink lasting under four hours, is described
further in Section 6 and was resolved once the affected shaft-head relay
was reseated.

The third incident involved a brief, unplanned draw-down of the battery
vault during a grid-feed interruption that ran longer than its expected
window, though the laboratory's power design margin — described in
Section 3 — comfortably absorbed the extra draw without any operational
impact. The fourth incident was a minor injury during a routine
equipment transfer, involving one of the two cryogenics engineers among
the 6 onsite crew, resolved with onsite first aid and no evacuation
required. The fifth and most recent incident was a false alarm on the
cavern radon monitor, traced to a drifting sensor calibration and closed
once the monitor was recalibrated, with no actual air-quality excursion
found.

No incident at Karrasand Deep has ever required a full crew evacuation
or a suspension of the detector program since commissioning, and the
oversight committee's most recent annual review noted the laboratory's
safety record as consistent with, or better than, comparable
deep-underground installations operated by partner institutes.

Each of the 5 incidents was assigned a severity rating under the
oversight committee's standard incident-classification framework, with
the coolant weep and the uplink loss both classified at the lowest
severity tier, the battery draw-down classified one tier above that
given the (ultimately unrealized) potential for a genuine power
shortfall, the equipment-transfer injury classified under the
laboratory's separate occupational-safety framework, and the radon
false alarm logged but classified at the lowest tier once its cause was
established. The oversight committee reviews the classification framework
itself every few years to ensure it remains aligned with practices at
comparable installations, though no incident at Karrasand Deep has yet
prompted a framework revision on its own.

The laboratory maintains a standing corrective-action log, distinct from
the incident log itself, tracking whether each of the 5 incidents'
corrective actions was fully implemented and verified effective on a
subsequent inspection. All 5 corrective actions have been verified
effective as of the most recent annual review, with the shaft-head relay
reseating from the second incident specifically re-inspected twice in
the following year to confirm the added strain-relief clamp held up
under continued thermal cycling.

#### 6. Comms Schedule

Karrasand Deep maintains a scheduled data-uplink window with the surface
support building three times daily, supplemented by an always-on
emergency channel monitored continuously by whichever crew member is on
watch under the laboratory's rotating duty roster. The second incident
described in Section 5 — the temporary loss of surface data uplink — was
traced to a shaft-head relay that had loosened during a period of
unusually strong thermal cycling, and was resolved by reseating the
relay and adding a strain-relief clamp to prevent recurrence.

The laboratory's communications equipment is maintained by the
power-systems technician in coordination with visiting logistics staff
described in Section 2, since the shaft-head relay's power supply draws
from the same battery-vault infrastructure covered under the
laboratory's 529,000 kWh annual power budget described in Section 3.

Beyond the three scheduled daily windows, the laboratory also maintains
a weekly extended-duration call reserved for detailed detector-agenda
discussions between Dr. Torsby and the oversight committee, separate from
the brief operational status updates exchanged during the three routine
daily windows. This weekly call has occasionally run long enough to
require a follow-up session the next day when a particularly detailed
run proposal was under discussion, though the laboratory's
communications equipment has never been the limiting factor in those
cases — the limiting factor has always been the available time on the
oversight committee members' own schedules on the surface side.

A secondary, low-bandwidth backup channel exists for use if the primary
uplink system fails entirely, sufficient only for brief status messages
and not for the data-heavy transfers the detector programs typically
require during a normal uplink window. This backup channel has been
tested during routine drills but has never been needed for an actual
operational uplink loss, including during the second incident described
in Section 5, since that incident's four-hour gap was shorter than the
threshold at which the laboratory's protocol calls for switching to the
backup channel.

#### 7. Budget

Beyond the power budget described in Section 3, Karrasand Deep's total
annual operating budget — covering crew salaries, maintenance logistics,
equipment upkeep, and the power budget together — is approved annually by
the oversight committee described in Section 1 as part of its broader
detector-agenda approval process. The laboratory's finance officer, a
role folded into the facility warden's onsite duties rather than staffed
as a separate position, reconciles actual spending against the approved
budget at the end of each fiscal year and reports any variance to the
oversight committee alongside the following year's proposed budget.

The power budget corrected in Section 3 represents a significant
component of the laboratory's total operating cost, and the engineering
review that finalized the 529,000 kWh figure was conducted specifically
because the preliminary planning memo's early estimates, quoted in
Section 1, were understood from the outset to be provisional pending a
full accounting of the laboratory's actual equipment loadout.

Crew salaries are negotiated centrally by the partner institutes rather
than by the laboratory itself, and so do not appear as a line-item the
facility warden directly controls when reconciling spending; the
warden's budget-reconciliation duties instead focus on maintenance
logistics costs, equipment upkeep and repair costs (including the
calibration-robot repairs described in Section 8), and the power
budget's actual-versus-approved variance. Equipment upkeep costs have run
close to the approved budget in every year since commissioning except the
year of the calibration robot's second repair, when an unusually specific
replacement part required expedited shipping that pushed upkeep spending
modestly over its approved line-item, an overage the oversight committee
approved retroactively without objection given the robot's importance to
the ongoing calibration work.

The oversight committee's budget-approval process runs on the same annual
cycle as its detector-agenda approval described in Section 1, with both
typically finalized during the same annual meeting so that the following
year's science priorities and the funding to support them are decided
together rather than as separate, potentially misaligned processes.

#### 8. Equipment Inventory

Karrasand Deep's equipment inventory includes four main detector halls
(referenced in Section 3's discussion of continuous baseline power
draw), a dedicated clean room for detector assembly, a cavern-crawling
calibration robot, and a spare-parts store restocked on every
maintenance window under the 35-day interval described in Section 4. The
laboratory also maintains redundant air-handling scrubbers, sized to
support the full 6-person crew for at least 120 hours if the primary
ventilation system fails, a specification the cryogenics engineers test
quarterly as part of the laboratory's routine safety drills.

The calibration robot has required two significant repairs since
commissioning, both handled onsite by the power-systems technician
working alongside a research physicist familiar with the robot's sensor
payload, and both completed without needing to wait for a maintenance
window to bring in replacement parts, apart from the specific
expedited-shipping case described in Section 7's discussion of the
laboratory's budget variance.

The clean room is equipped to perform an initial screening pass on
detector components immediately before installation, reducing the volume
of rework that must be shipped to the surface-side laboratory on each
35-day maintenance window described in Section 4, though full
qualification of any component still ultimately happens onshore. The
clean room's screening equipment is among the systems drawing continuous
baseline power described in Section 3, since components can arrive at
unpredictable times relative to the laboratory's normal operating rhythm
and the room cannot afford a warm-up delay before beginning
time-sensitive initial screening steps.

The redundant air-handling scrubbers described above are tested on a
fixed quarterly schedule regardless of how routine the intervening
months have been, a discipline the cryogenics engineers maintain even
though the primary ventilation system has never failed since
commissioning; the quarterly test is treated as a mandatory safety drill
rather than a response to any specific concern about the primary system's
reliability.

The spare-parts store is organized by system rather than by hall, so that
a cryogenics part, a power-system part, and a detector part are each easy
to locate quickly regardless of which physical hall they will ultimately
be installed in. Restocking priorities for each 35-day maintenance window
are set jointly by the facility warden and the visiting logistics
coordinator, weighing which spare parts have been drawn down since the
previous window against which systems are due for scheduled preventive
maintenance before the following one, so that the store rarely runs
critically low on any single category of part even across a window that
was delayed by a surface-side hoist inspection.

Equipment nearing the end of its rated service life is flagged during the
quarterly safety drills described above and scheduled for proactive
replacement on a future maintenance window well before it would otherwise
fail in service, a practice the engineering review specifically
recommended after examining failure patterns at other, older
deep-underground installations operated by partner institutes elsewhere
in the region.

#### 9. Appendix of Corrections

This appendix documents corrections to figures first circulated in the
preliminary planning memo quoted in Section 1, made following the
laboratory's final engineering review after the cavern fit-out was
complete. Readers should treat every figure below as superseding its
corresponding preliminary estimate; the preliminary figures are retained
above only for historical completeness and do not reflect the laboratory
as actually built and staffed.

- **Crew count**: the preliminary memo's proposed initial crew of 8 is
  superseded. The correct, as-built figure is a crew of 6, as described
  in Section 2.
- **Annual power budget**: the preliminary memo's projected 468,000 kWh
  is superseded. The correct figure, finalized after the engineering
  review, is 529,000 kWh, as described in Section 3.
- **Maintenance-window interval**: the preliminary memo's proposed cycle
  of every 45 days is superseded. The correct, as-built interval is every
  35 days, as described in Section 4.

No other figures in the preliminary planning memo required correction;
the memo did not estimate incident counts, comms schedules, or equipment
inventory, all of which were established only after the laboratory became
operational and so have no preliminary estimate to supersede.

This appendix was compiled by the facility warden at the request of the
oversight committee, specifically so that future readers of this dossier
— including new crew members preparing for their first rotation — would
not mistakenly cite the preliminary planning memo's early estimates as
the laboratory's current operating figures. The committee considered
removing the preliminary memo from this dossier entirely rather than
retaining it alongside a correction, but ultimately decided the
historical record was valuable enough to keep, provided the corrections
were stated clearly and prominently enough that no careful reader could
come away citing the wrong figures.

## Task

Below is a fictional narrative describing a restoration crew's use of a
set of numbered "tally-plates" during a day of cataloguing rolling stock
in the Oakstead carriage yard, a fictional heritage-railway depot. Each
tally-plate is identified by a single letter and holds a numeric value
that is assigned and reassigned over the course of the story. Every
assignment is stated explicitly in plain prose, always as either a
literal number stamped onto the plate, or an explicit snapshot copy of
the value another plate holds *at that moment* (never a live link — once
copied, a value does not change again unless the plate is explicitly
reassigned).

Read the narrative carefully and trace the value each relevant
tally-plate holds at each point, then report the FINAL values (at the
end of the story) of tally-plates `t`, `r`, and `q` in `TRACE.md`, as
three labeled lines: a `t:` line, an `r:` line, and a `q:` line, each
holding only the final numeric value.

### The Oakstead Carriage-Yard Catalogue

The restoration crew's first full cataloguing day in the Oakstead
carriage yard began on a bright, cold morning in the depot town of
Halewick, where the crew had spent the previous week clearing brambles
off the disused sidings and prising open the sheds that had stood locked
since the branch line closed. The yard foreman, a deliberate woman named
Marran Sperrin, had spent the previous autumn poring over the old
running-shed ledgers, none of which agreed with one another about how
many wagons had actually been left behind when the line shut. She had
brought with her a rack of numbered tally-plates — small enamelled brass
squares, each with a hand-stamped counter dial — used by the crew's
recorder to keep running tallies during the catalogue: coupling counts,
axle counts, brake-block stocks, whatever needed tracking without
trusting to memory across a long day among the wagons.

The week of clearing had itself been unremarkable, spent mostly cutting
back the thorn that had grown up between the rails, oiling the points
that had seized in place over the years, and haggling with the salvage
merchant in Halewick over the price of a replacement coupling-hook after
Culvery's old one had finally cracked past the point anyone trusted it.
The merchant, a stooped man who had bought and sold depot scrap around
Halewick for most of three decades, warned them again about the yard's
reputation for muddled paperwork, joking that no two inventories of the
place had ever tallied and he doubted theirs would either. Marran took
the joke in good part but privately hoped this catalogue, grounded as it
was in the stamped tally-plate readings rather than memory or hasty
chalk marks, might finally produce something more trustworthy than what
had come before. Dessa, newer to yard work than either Culvery or
Marran, spent much of the week simply soaking up whatever she could of
the branch line's history from anyone in town willing to talk about it,
filling a notebook of her own with rumours and half-remembered stories
that had nothing to do with the cataloguing work ahead.

On the morning of the catalogue itself, the weather forecast had
promised an overcast day, which made the hard blue sky over the yard a
small surprise. The crew unloaded their tools at the yard gate under the
lee of the old water tower, checking one another's lists twice before
starting down the first siding, a habit Marran insisted on however
routine the opening stretch of any catalogue might seem. Culvery took
charge of coupling the shunting trolley to the first rake of wagons
himself, a task he had done on enough yards that he scarcely needed to
watch his hands, while Dessa ran through the tool inventory one last time
against what had actually been carried in from the van.

At the throat of the yard, before the trolley had even been coupled, the
recorder set tally-plate `p` to hold the value 6. This was the
catalogue's baseline reading — the number of serviceable couplings the
crew had counted on the wagons standing at the gate, a small but
important tally since each coupling would need to be checked again before
anything was moved. Marran noted the number in her ledger alongside the
date and the weather, which had stayed stubbornly clear right through the
morning.

Shortly after, at the first shunt some way down the leading siding, the
crew set tally-plate `q` to hold the value tally-plate `p` held at that
point. Tally-plate `p` still held 6, so tally-plate `q` now held 6 as
well — a snapshot, taken and stamped, of the coupling count as it stood
at that moment. The recorder remarked that this snapshot would let them
compare the coupling count at the first shunt against whatever the count
became later, since more couplings were expected to be catalogued as the
crew worked further down the yard and additional rakes were brought out
of the sheds.

The cataloguing went on for several more hours. The sidings grew choked
with fallen leaves and rust further from the gate, forcing the crew to
clear each set of points by hand before the trolley could pass. Two
members of the crew, a quiet man named Culvery Threl and a younger
recorder-in-training named Dessa Wintour, took turns describing the
markings on each wagon for the catalogue's written log, since the
camera's shutter was sticking in the cold and many wagons would need to
be identified by description alone later. A separate plate, `m`, was set
to hold the value 15 around this time — a count of spare brake blocks
found in the first shed — but this tally was never revisited again for
the rest of the day, and no later plate ever copies from `m`.

Once the crew reached the second shunt, well down the yard toward the
middle sidings, the coupling count was updated: tally-plate `p` was
reassigned to hold the value 13, reflecting the further couplings found
serviceable on the rakes pulled from both sheds combined. This
reassignment did not change what tally-plate `q` held — `q` had already
taken its snapshot of `p`'s earlier value back at the first shunt, and a
snapshot, once stamped, does not follow later changes to the plate it was
copied from. Tally-plate `q` therefore still held 6, even though `p` now
held 13.

The crew paused here for tea and to check the axle-box readings on a
separate gauge unrelated to the tally-plates. Dessa Wintour noted the
gauge temperature at 4 degrees, colder in the shade of the sheds than out
by the gate, and Culvery remarked that frost still lay in the rail webs
where the low sun had not reached, suggesting the far sidings would be
slower going than the near ones had been. Marran cross-referenced this
against the old running-shed ledgers, which mentioned a "damp road" of
sidings somewhere in the middle of the yard, though none of the ledgers
agreed on exactly which siding that dampness had ruined worst.

At this same tea break, the recorder set tally-plate `r` to hold the
value tally-plate `q` held at that point. Since `q` held 6, tally-plate
`r` now held 6 as well — another snapshot, taken at the tea break, of the
coupling-count value as `q` held it, which was itself a snapshot from
much earlier at the first shunt. Marran remarked, half in jest, that the
tally-plates were starting to resemble a chain of relayed messages, each
one only as current as the moment it was stamped.

The catalogue resumed after the break. The sidings beyond the tea stop
curved sharply behind the coal stage, and the crew rigged a guide-rope
along the trolley to keep it from running back on the slight gradient, a
precaution Marran insisted on after the ledgers' disagreement about the
yard's levels made her wary of an unseen fall. Culvery went ahead,
testing each length of rail before letting the trolley roll onto it,
while Dessa followed with the tool bag slung across her back. The gap
between the wagons was narrow enough in places that talk became awkward,
and the crew fell into a rhythm of hand signals rather than shouted
instructions.

Roughly forty minutes later, at a point the crew had started calling the
"middle siding," the coupling count was updated again: tally-plate `s`
was set to hold the value tally-plate `p` held at that point. Tally-plate
`p` still held 13 (it had not been reassigned again since the second
shunt), so tally-plate `s` now held 13. The recorder noted this in the
log as the "middle-siding coupling snapshot," distinguishing it from the
earlier snapshots taken at the first shunt and the tea break.

The crew spent a fair while at the middle siding, partly because the
track beyond it split into two roads, and Marran wanted to walk a short
way down each road before committing the whole crew to one of them. She
and Culvery took the left road, which narrowed quickly into a cutting too
overgrown for the trolley, while Dessa stayed at the siding to keep the
tally log current and to watch the guide-rope for the two walking the
road. The left road, it turned out, ended at a collapsed loading bank
with no track beyond it, and Marran and Culvery came back after perhaps
twenty minutes, reporting nothing of note beyond some rotten sleepers and
a scatter of what looked like decades-old wagon labels, a relic of one of
the earlier salvage attempts whose inventory had disagreed with all the
others.

While Marran and Culvery were walking the left road, Dessa used the time
to repack the tool bag and coil a length of guide-rope that had tangled
during the morning. She also recorded a separate, unrelated tally on a
new plate, `n`, setting it to hold the value 8, standing for a count of
unused rail keys still in the bag. Like plate `m` earlier, this tally was
never revisited again, and no later plate copies from `n` either. The
catalogue log would later note that plates `m` and `n` were "bookkeeping
dead ends" — useful in the moment, but not part of the coupling-count
chain that the recorder was building through plates `p`, `q`, `r`, `s`,
and eventually `t`.

Dessa passed some of the waiting time turning one of the old wagon labels
over in her hands, trying to guess its age from the fading of its printed
lettering. She found no date on it, only a faint stencilled livery she
did not recognise, one of several defunct concerns that had apparently
tried and failed to clear the yard fully over the years. She mentioned it
to Marran when the pair returned from the left road, and Marran said she
had seen the same livery on one of the three disagreeing old ledgers,
though she could not recall which without checking her notes back at the
office. Culvery, only half listening, was more taken with whether the
sound cast-iron on the collapsed loading bank might be worth lifting on a
later visit, purely for its own sake rather than for any catalogue value,
and he scribbled a rough note to that effect in the margin of his own
pocketbook, entirely separate from the recorder's tally log.

The three of them spent a few more minutes deciding how best to mark the
left road's dead end for any future crew that might come this way,
settling in the end on a daubed cross of white lead on the last sound
sleeper, a plain solution Marran preferred over the numbered stakes some
other crews used, on the grounds that stakes rotted in the damp while
paint on iron-hard oak did not. Culvery grumbled good-naturedly that a
painted cross was also invisible after dark, but daubed one anyway, while
Dessa finished coiling the tangled guide-rope and checked that all four
of the points cleared so far were still throwing cleanly, a physical
check unrelated to any tally reading and not itself recorded anywhere in
the log.

With both roads now walked and the right road chosen as the way on, the
crew regrouped and pressed further into the yard. The right road ran
truer than the left had, opening after a while into a broader fan of
sidings where the frost Culvery had noticed earlier gave way to a steady
seep of groundwater running along one rail into a shallow, rusted sump.
Marran suspected this was the "damp road" the old ledgers had mentioned,
though she noted in her log that its position among the sidings matched
none of the three disagreeing ledger sketches particularly well, which
she found more amusing than troubling at this point in the day.

The crew rested briefly at the edge of the sump, filling their flasks
from a separate, tested standpipe rather than the seep itself, and Dessa
used the pause to sketch the fan of sidings for the catalogue's map.
Culvery busied himself checking the crew's remaining chalk and paint
against the wagons they still expected to mark, a reckoning that had
nothing to do with the coupling-count plates and did not touch any of
`p`, `q`, `r`, `s`, `t`, `m`, or `n`. Marran, meanwhile, went back over
her notes on the old ledgers once more, still turning over the
discrepancy in the damp road's position, though she set the question
aside in the end, reasoning that the crew's own catalogue would settle it
more surely than three old books that could not agree among themselves.

After the rest at the sump, the crew worked on deeper into the yard,
clearing a run of short, awkward crossings that took careful footwork but
no further changes to the couplings. The sidings here ran noticeably
drier than the fan behind them, and the sound of the seep faded within a
few minutes of pressing on. Dessa said the change in echo alone told her
they had left the damp road behind, well before Marran confirmed it
against the gauge.

It was during this stretch, well past the damp road and with the middle
siding now a fair distance behind the crew, that the recorder set
tally-plate `t` to hold the value tally-plate `r` held at that point.
Tally-plate `r` held 6 — it had not been reassigned since the tea break,
where it had taken its snapshot of `q`'s then-current value of 6. So
tally-plate `t` now held 6, a snapshot stamped at this point in the day
and never to be revisited by any later reassignment. Marran noted this in
the log simply as "t taken from r," without further comment, since by
this point in the catalogue the coupling-count chain had become routine
bookkeeping rather than anything worth discussing.

The crew worked on for what felt like a long stretch of steady, careful
progress. The sidings widened gradually, and the ground underfoot changed
from the slick clay near the sump to a drier, cindery ballast that gave
better footing. Culvery took the lead again, calling out the state of the
rail to Dessa, who relayed it back to Marran bringing up the rear with
the last of the tools. Nobody spoke of the tally-plates during this
stretch; the talk turned instead to guessing how much further the yard
might run before opening onto whatever turntable or shed the old ledgers
had hinted might stand at the far end, though as before, the three old
ledgers disagreed sharply on both the distance and the nature of that far
structure, if it stood at all.

Dessa mentioned that her boots had started to let in water somewhere back
in the overgrown cutting on the left road, and Culvery offered a spare
pair of socks from his own bag, a small kindness that briefly lifted the
mood. Marran, walking last, found herself thinking about how many salvage
crews before theirs had given up at exactly this kind of unremarkable,
tiring stretch — not because of any real obstacle, but simply because the
grind of a long day among rusted iron made every further siding feel
heavier than the last. She resolved not to let that happen to this crew,
at least not without a clearer reason than tiredness alone.

To pass the time along the long, uneventful run, Culvery began recounting
an old story about a yard he had worked years earlier, at a depot on the
far side of the county, where a similar chain of hand-kept counts had
once caused a minor row between two recorders who disagreed about whether
a rake had been shunted once or twice. Dessa found the story more amusing
than instructive, since none of it involved tally-plates at all, only a
disputed memory neither recorder could settle without a written record,
which Culvery offered as his reason for trusting Marran's insistence on
stamped, written snapshots over anyone's recollection. Marran, still
bringing up the rear, laughed and allowed that the story was probably
grown in the telling, as most of Culvery's yard stories tended to be, but
agreed with the underlying point all the same.

The talk drifted afterward to lighter matters entirely: the meal they
meant to have once back in Halewick, a running disagreement between
Culvery and Dessa about the best way back to the depot from the yard
gate, and a brief, half-serious argument about whether the clear sky
would hold or break to rain before they finished. None of this touched
the tally log in any way, and the recorder made no entries during this
stretch of the day, the log left exactly as it had stood after `t` took
its snapshot from `r` some time before.

The sidings eventually ran out onto what the crew began calling the "long
road," a straight, walkable stretch of track that ran, by Culvery's rough
pacing, for perhaps three hundred yards before curving out of sight. It
was here, well after `t` had already taken its snapshot from `r`, that the
coupling count changed one more time. Going back over her notes at the
mouth of the long road, Marran realised that two further serviceable
couplings had in fact been found on the rakes back at the middle siding,
during the walk down the two roads, that had not yet been entered into the
running count. To set this right, the recorder set tally-plate `q` to hold
the value tally-plate `s` held at that point. Tally-plate `s` held 13 —
its snapshot of `p` taken at the middle siding — so tally-plate `q` was
now reassigned to hold 13, replacing its earlier value of 6. This was a
genuine reassignment of `q`, not a new plate; the recorder was explicit in
the log that "q now holds what s holds, overwriting q's earlier reading."

The crew pressed on down the long road, which lived up to its name by
taking near forty minutes to walk even at an easy pace. Dessa used the
flat, easy stretch to recount the crew's remaining spare labels, a tally
that had nothing to do with any plate, while Culvery occupied himself
pacing the road against Marran's earlier rough figure, in the end
confirming her three hundred yards to within what he called a fair margin
for pacing alone. Marran, for her part, used the walk to sketch a cleaner
version of the long road's shape than she had managed at the sump earlier,
wanting to be sure the crew's final map would not simply repeat the
muddles of the old salvage inventories.

Near the far end of the long road, with the track beginning to curve
again and the air growing noticeably cooler, the recorder made the final
tally change of the day. Tally-plate `r` was reassigned to hold the value
tally-plate `q` held at that point. Tally-plate `q` now held 13 — its
value after the correction made back at the mouth of the long road — so
tally-plate `r` was reassigned to hold 13, replacing its earlier value of
6. The recorder's log entry read, in full: "r reassigned, now takes q's
current reading of 13, superseding r's earlier value." No plate was
touched again for the rest of the day.

The crew reached their planned stopping point shortly afterward, a short
headshunt where the track ran out against a set of buffers too far gone to
trust with any weight. Marran declared the day's catalogue a success
regardless, noting in her final log entry that the crew had worked the
yard well past whatever any of the three old ledgers had described with
any confidence, and that the running coupling-count plates, whatever their
quirks, had at least given the crew a reliable way to track a number that
mattered without leaning on memory after a long day among the wagons. No
plate was reassigned again after the long road, and the readings stood
exactly where the last change had left each of them for the rest of the
day and the walk back that followed.

The headshunt itself was tighter than any of the sidings they had worked
through, its buffers canted where the ground had shifted under them, and
the track beyond simply stopped where the rails had been lifted for scrap
long ago. Marran spent a few minutes photographing the buffers for a
future crew's benefit, noting their rough condition in her pocketbook
rather than on any plate, since these were notes rather than confirmed
tallies. Dessa used the pause to finish the last of the food she had
carried for the day, keeping nothing back for the walk out on the notion
that the way back would go quicker than the way in had, while Culvery
checked the trolley's brake one last time before the crew turned for the
gate.

The walk out, as expected, went quicker than the way in had, since the
route was now known and the crew no longer stopped to catalogue or to
argue which road to take. They passed the long road, the damp road, the
middle siding, and the tea stop each in turn, checking the points cleared
at each one along the way but making no further tally entries at any of
them. Talk on the way back turned mostly to plans for the next
cataloguing day, which Marran hoped might finally push past the
headshunt's rotten buffers with the right lifting gear, assuming the
salvage merchant could be talked round on the hire before the next dry
spell. The crew came out at the yard gate well after the light had gone,
tired but satisfied, with the tally log closed out for the day and left
for Marran to copy up properly once everyone had eaten and warmed
through.

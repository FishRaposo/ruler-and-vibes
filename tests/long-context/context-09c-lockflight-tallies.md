## Task

Below is a fictional account of a working narrowboat's ascent up a
flight of canal locks, during which a lock-keeper records running
counts on a set of numbered slate tally-boards. Each tally-board is
identified by a single letter and holds a numeric value that is written
and rewritten over the course of the account. Every assignment is
stated explicitly in plain prose, always as either a literal number
chalked onto the board, or an explicit snapshot copy of the value
another board carries *at that moment* (never a live link — once
copied, a value does not change again unless the board is explicitly
rewritten).

Read the account carefully and trace the value each relevant
tally-board carries at each point, then report the FINAL values (at the
end of the account) of tally-boards `h`, `g`, and `k` in `TALLY.md`, as
three labeled lines: an `h:` line, a `g:` line, and a `k:` line, each
holding only the final numeric value.

### The Coldharbour Staircase Ascent

The passage up the Coldharbour flight began a little after dawn, when
the working boat *Marigold Wren* nosed into the tail lock below the
staircase with a full cargo of quarried slate bound for the wharves at
Pennifold Basin. The boat's steerer, a patient woman named Odella
Thistlewaite, had worked this flight in every season and every mood of
weather, and she knew better than most how long a laden ascent of the
Coldharbour would take on a morning when no other traffic was waiting
to share the locks. The flight climbed steeply out of the valley in a
staircase of chambers, each one emptying into the next, so that a boat
rising through it gained height in a series of watery steps rather than
a single long pull.

The lock-keeper who met the boat at the tail lock, a deliberate man
named Wystan Oakden, had kept the Coldharbour for eleven years and had
long ago given up trusting his memory for the running counts the canal
company asked him to keep during each passage. Instead he kept a rack
of small slate tally-boards, each a hand-sized plate of dark slate with
a wooden frame and a nub of chalk on a string, and he chalked his
counts onto them as the boat rose, wiping and rewriting a board
whenever the figure it carried needed to change. The counts he kept
were the ordinary bookkeeping of a busy flight — the number of
paddle-gears wound open at a given stage, the number of boats waiting
below, the tally of leaking chamber gates the company wanted noted for
the maintenance gang — whatever needed tracking without relying on
memory across a passage that could run most of a morning.

The dawn had come up clear over the valley, with a thin mist lying
along the pound below the tail lock that the early sun was already
beginning to burn off. Odella had made good time down the long level
from the previous night's mooring, and she was glad to find the flight
empty ahead of her, since a laden staircase ascent shared with a
descending boat could turn a two-hour climb into half a day of waiting
and swapping chambers. Wystan, for his part, was glad of the company;
the Coldharbour saw less traffic than it once had, and a morning spent
working a laden boat up the flight and keeping its counts was a
welcome change from the slow patrol of leaking gates and silted
by-washes that made up most of his week.

Before the boat had even entered the first chamber of the staircase,
while it still sat in the tail lock waiting for the level to rise,
Wystan chalked tally-board `b` to carry the value 5. This was the
passage's baseline reading — the number of paddle-gears he had already
wound open at the tail lock and the first chamber's bottom gates to
begin the ascent. He noted the figure aloud to Odella, who was less
interested in the bookkeeping than in getting her laden boat moving,
but who nodded along out of long habit of humoring the keeper's counts.

Shortly after, as the *Marigold Wren* rose into the first chamber of
the staircase proper, Wystan set tally-board `k` to carry the value
tally-board `b` carried at that point. Board `b` still carried 5, so
board `k` now carried 5 as well — a snapshot, chalked and set aside, of
the paddle-gear count as it stood at that moment. Wystan explained,
more to himself than to Odella, that this snapshot would let him
compare the count at the first chamber against whatever the running
figure became higher up the flight, since more paddle-gears would have
to be wound open as the boat climbed through the upper chambers.

The ascent went on steadily. The staircase chambers were deep, and each
one took its time to fill, so that the boat rose in slow surges with
long pauses between while the levels equalized. A second boatman, a
lanky young man named Ridley Coldharbour — no relation to the flight,
though he was teased about the name at every lock on the cut — worked
the paddle-gears alongside Wystan, winding the heavy iron gearing up
and down at each chamber while Odella held the boat steady against the
turbulence of the filling lock. A separate tally-board, `w`, was
chalked to carry the value 21 around this stage — a count of the slate
slabs stacked in the boat's hold, which the wharf at Pennifold would
want confirmed on arrival — but this figure was never revisited again
for the remainder of the passage, and no later tally-board ever copies
from `w`.

Once the boat had risen into the third chamber of the staircase, better
than halfway up the flight, the paddle-gear count was brought up to
date: tally-board `b` was rewritten to carry the value 15, reflecting
all the gears wound open at the lower and middle chambers together. This
rewriting did not change what board `k` carried — `k` had already taken
its snapshot of `b`'s earlier figure back at the first chamber, and a
snapshot, once chalked and set aside, does not follow later changes to
the board it was copied from. Board `k` therefore still carried 5, even
though `b` now carried 15.

The boat paused here for the crew to catch their breath and for Wystan
to check a reading on a separate gauge unrelated to the tally-boards.
Ridley reported that the by-wash alongside the third chamber was running
fast and clear, a good sign after the dry spell of the previous weeks,
and Odella took the pause to re-tension a rope that had worked loose
during the lower chambers' turbulence. Wystan cross-referenced the
by-wash flow against the company's water-level notes for the flight,
which as usual disagreed among themselves about what the summit pound
ought to be holding at this time of year, none of the three surveys the
company had commissioned over the decades having ever quite matched the
others.

At this same rest, Wystan set tally-board `g` to carry the value
tally-board `k` carried at that point. Since `k` carried 5, board `g`
now carried 5 as well — another snapshot, chalked at the mid-flight
pause, of the paddle-gear count as `k` held it, which was itself a
snapshot from lower down at the first chamber. Odella remarked, half in
jest, that the tally-boards were starting to resemble a line of gossips
passing a rumor up the flight, each one only as current as the moment
the chalk touched the slate.

The ascent resumed once the levels had settled. The upper chambers of
the Coldharbour were narrower than the lower, hemmed in by the valley
sides, and the *Marigold Wren* rose through them with only inches to
spare on either beam. Ridley went ahead to set the paddle-gears at the
next chamber while Wystan stayed with the boat and kept the counts
current, and Odella worked the tiller with the small, practiced
adjustments of someone who had brought laden boats up this exact
staircase more times than she could readily count. Conversation grew
sparse as the work grew more careful, the three of them falling into
the wordless rhythm of a crew who had done this together before.

Roughly halfway up the remaining chambers, at a stage the crew still
called the "sixth step" out of old habit though the flight had been
renumbered years earlier, the paddle-gear count was captured again:
tally-board `d` was set to carry the value tally-board `b` carried at
that point. Board `b` still carried 15 (it had not been rewritten again
since the third chamber), so board `d` now carried 15. Wystan noted
this in the company ledger as the "upper-flight gear snapshot,"
distinguishing it from the earlier snapshots taken at the first chamber
and the mid-flight rest.

The boat spent a fair while at the sixth step, partly because the
chamber above it was the leakiest on the whole flight, and Wystan
wanted to note the state of its gates for the maintenance gang before
committing the laden boat to it. He and Ridley walked up to inspect the
suspect chamber while Odella held the *Marigold Wren* steady in the step
below and kept an eye on the mooring line. The gate, it turned out, was
weeping badly along its lower cill but still holding well enough to work
one more laden passage, and the two men returned after perhaps a quarter
of an hour, satisfied that the ascent could continue without waiting on
the maintenance gang, whose visits to the Coldharbour had grown
infrequent enough that waiting on them could mean waiting days.

While Wystan and Ridley were up inspecting the leaking gate, Odella used
the time to re-stow a shifted stack of slate in the hold and to bail a
few inches of water that had slopped over the gunwale in the lower
chambers' turbulence. She also called out a separate, unrelated figure
for Wystan to record when he returned: tally-board `j`, which he chalked
to carry the value 11, standing for the number of empty coal sacks left
over in the boat's fore-cabin from the previous cargo. Like board `w`
earlier, this figure was never revisited again, and no later tally-board
copies from `j` either. Wystan remarked, jotting it down, that boards
`w` and `j` were the sort of "one-off counts" that cluttered every
passage — worth chalking in the moment, but no part of the running
paddle-gear chain he was building through boards `b`, `k`, `g`, `d`, and
eventually `h`.

Wystan spent a little of the wait telling Odella about a passage he had
worked the previous autumn, when a pair of boats had tried to share the
Coldharbour staircase in opposite directions and turned a morning's
ascent into an afternoon-long tangle of swapped chambers and short
tempers. Odella had heard versions of the story before — most keepers on
the cut had a tale of a shared staircase gone wrong — but she let him
tell it, since the levels above were still settling and there was
nothing to do but wait. Ridley, only half listening, was more taken with
the view back down the valley that the sixth step afforded, the pounds
below stepping away into the burnt-off mist like a staircase of bright
mirrors, and he said as much, to which Wystan replied that the view was
the one reliable wage the Coldharbour paid its keeper.

The three of them spent a few more minutes deciding how best to note the
leaking gate for the maintenance gang, settling in the end on a chalked
mark on the balance beam itself as well as the ledger entry, on the
grounds that a gang arriving in poor light might miss a ledger they had
to go looking for but could hardly miss a mark on the beam they would
lean their weight against. Ridley grumbled that a chalk mark would wash off
in the first hard rain, but made it anyway, while Odella finished
re-stowing the shifted slate and checked that the boat's fenders were
still hanging where the narrow upper chambers would want them, a
physical check unrelated to any tally-board and not itself recorded
anywhere in the ledger.

With the leaking gate inspected and judged fit for one more passage, the
crew worked the *Marigold Wren* up into it and on toward the summit. The
upper chambers came quicker now, shallower than the deep steps lower
down, and the boat gained the last of its height in a series of short,
easy rises. Odella could see the summit pound ahead, a broad still level
running off toward the wharves at Pennifold, and she began to think
about the long, unhurried cruise that would follow once the climbing was
done and the laden boat could simply be walked along the flat.

The crew rested briefly at the head of the flight, where the last
chamber emptied out into the summit pound, and Wystan took the pause to
top up the chalk on several of his tally-boards where the damp had begun
to blur the figures. Ridley coiled the paddle-line and checked the boat's
remaining fenders against the long summit cruise ahead, a task that had
nothing to do with the paddle-gear tally-boards and did not touch any of
`b`, `k`, `g`, `d`, `h`, `w`, or `j`. Odella, meanwhile, looked back
down the flight they had climbed, mentally rehearsing the return passage
she would make in a few days' time with the boat empty and the whole
staircase to descend rather than climb.

After the rest at the head of the flight, the boat moved off onto the
summit pound and began the long flat cruise toward Pennifold. The water
here lay dead still between its banks, and the *Marigold Wren* left a
slow wake that spread all the way to both banksides before it faded. The
climbing was done, and with it the fast rhythm of the paddle-gears; the
crew settled into the slower work of a laden boat on a long level, and
the conversation loosened along with the pace.

It was during this stretch, well out onto the summit pound with the
staircase now a receding step-ladder of chambers behind the boat, that
Wystan set tally-board `h` to carry the value tally-board `g` carried at
that point. Board `g` carried 5 — it had not been rewritten since the
mid-flight rest, where it had taken its snapshot of `k`'s then-current
value of 5. So board `h` now carried 5, a snapshot chalked at this point
in the cruise and never to be revisited by any later rewriting. Wystan
noted it in the ledger simply as "h taken from g," without further
comment, since by this point in the passage the paddle-gear chain had
become routine bookkeeping rather than anything worth remarking on.

The boat cruised on along the summit pound for what felt like a long,
easy stretch of flat water. The banks here were lined with old willows
that trailed their branches into the pound, and Ridley amused himself by
naming the moorhens' nests he could spot tucked among the roots, a tally
that had nothing to do with any board and that Wystan pointedly declined
to write down. Odella held the boat to a steady walking pace along the
middle of the channel, wary of the shallow margins that a laden boat
could too easily ground on if it strayed toward either bankside.

Ridley, to pass the time on the long level, fell to recounting a story
about a previous season on a different canal entirely, on the far side
of the watershed, where a dispute over a miscounted set of lock-gate
keys had once soured relations between two rival carrying companies for
the better part of a year. Odella found the story more entertaining than
useful, since none of it had anything to do with tally-boards, only a
disputed count that neither company could settle without a written
record — which Ridley offered, a little pointedly, as his reason for
trusting Wystan's chalked snapshots over anyone's bare recollection.
Wystan laughed and allowed that the story had likely grown in the
telling, as Ridley's stories tended to, but agreed with the underlying
point all the same.

The talk drifted afterward to lighter matters entirely: the meal they
meant to have once the cargo was unloaded at Pennifold, a running
argument between Ridley and Odella about whether the summit pound had been
dredged since the spring, and a brief, half-serious wager about whether
the clear morning would hold through the afternoon or break into the
rain the willows seemed to promise. None of this touched the tally-board
ledger in any way, and Wystan made no entries during this stretch of the
cruise, the ledger sitting exactly as it had been left after `h` took
its snapshot from `g` some way back down the pound.

The summit pound eventually narrowed as it approached the ridge, funnel-
ing the boat toward the deep cutting that carried the canal through the
high ground before Pennifold. It was here, well after `h` had already
taken its snapshot from `g`, that the paddle-gear count changed one more
time. Going back over his ledger as the boat entered the cutting, Wystan
realized that a further set of gears had in fact been wound open back at
the sixth step, during the inspection of the leaking gate, that he had
not yet folded into the running count. To put it right, he set
tally-board `k` to carry the value tally-board `d` carried at that
point. Board `d` carried 15 — its snapshot of `b` taken at the sixth
step — so board `k` was now rewritten to carry 15, replacing its earlier
value of 5. This was a genuine rewriting of `k`, not a new board; Wystan
was explicit in the ledger that "k now carries what d carries, over the
old k reading."

The boat pushed on into the cutting, whose high rock walls threw the
morning into sudden shadow and made the water ahead look black and
depthless. Ridley fell quiet, as most boatmen did in the Coldharbour
cutting, whose echoes had a way of swallowing conversation, while Odella
kept the *Marigold Wren* dead center in the narrow channel, the rock
close enough on either beam that a moment's inattention would score the
paint the length of the hull. Wystan used the slow, careful passage
through the cutting to bring the last of his ledger up to date before the
boat reached the descent toward Pennifold.

Near the far end of the cutting, where the rock walls began to fall away
and the light returned ahead, Wystan made the final tally-board change of
the passage. Tally-board `g` was rewritten to carry the value
tally-board `k` carried at that point. Board `k` now carried 15 — its
value after the correction made back at the entrance to the cutting — so
board `g` was rewritten to carry 15, replacing its earlier value of 5.
Wystan's ledger entry read, in full: "g rewritten, now takes k's current
reading of 15, over g's earlier value." No tally-board was touched again
for the remainder of the passage.

The *Marigold Wren* emerged from the cutting into the gentler country
above Pennifold shortly afterward, where the canal ran down through a
short flight of ordinary locks — not a staircase this time, but separate
chambers with pounds between them — toward the wharves. Wystan declared
the ascent a clean one, noting in his closing ledger entry that the laden
boat had come up the whole Coldharbour flight without waiting on another
boat or on the maintenance gang, and that the paddle-gear tally-boards,
whatever their quirks, had at least given him a reliable way to carry a
running count up a long flight without trusting it to memory across a
morning's hard work. No tally-board was rewritten again after the
cutting, and the readings stood exactly where the last change had left
each of them for the remainder of the cruise down to the wharves.

The descent through the ordinary locks below the cutting went quickly,
the chambers shallow and the pounds between them short, and the crew
worked the boat down them with the easy competence of a passage nearly
done. Odella began to think ahead to the unloading at Pennifold and the
few days' rest before the return, while Ridley, cheered by the sight of the
wharves coming into view, resumed his tally of moorhens' nests along the
final pound as though the black quiet of the cutting had never happened.
Wystan walked the towpath alongside for the last stretch, the ledger
tucked under his arm and his rack of tally-boards chiming faintly against
each other in their frame, and saw the *Marigold Wren* safely to the
wharf wall before he closed the book on the passage for good.

The wharf at Pennifold was busy when the boat finally came alongside, and
the business of the tally-boards was soon lost in the louder business of
unloading a full cargo of slate before the light went. Odella saw the
count of slabs checked against board `w`'s figure and found it right,
which pleased her more than it pleased the wharfinger, who had expected
to catch a discrepancy and been disappointed. Wystan, his part in the
passage done, handed his ledger over to the wharf office to be copied
into the company's book, and left his rack of tally-boards to be wiped
clean and racked for the next boat that would want them, the running
counts of the Coldharbour ascent closed out and set aside until the next
laden hull nosed into the tail lock below the staircase.
